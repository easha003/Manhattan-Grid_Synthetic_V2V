# Full-paper research notes: workbook entries P1–P8

These notes were prepared from the primary full texts, not from the short descriptions in
`RF_VLC_RL_literature_review.xlsx`. They are intended as a durable technical reference for the
planned Manhattan-grid NR-sidelink + vehicle-to-vehicle visible-light communication (V-VLC)
simulator and its constrained-RL controller. Workbook entry P7 contains two distinct papers, so
this file covers nine papers in total.

## Evidence convention

- **Direct** means that the statement is reported by, or follows immediately from an equation,
  table, or figure in, the source.
- **Calculated** means a simple calculation or approximate graph reading from reported material;
  it is not an exact number printed by the authors.
- **Inference / transfer** means an engineering conclusion for the planned simulator. It is not a
  claim made by the source.
- **Caveat** records missing detail, an inconsistency, or a limitation that matters when reusing
  the work.

All URLs were checked on 2026-07-28. Where a publisher copy was closed, the access route and the
relationship of the readable manuscript to the cited work are stated explicitly.

## Fast bibliographic and interpretation corrections to the workbook

1. **P1:** the five-page IEEE paper is paywalled, but its first author reproduces and expands the
   work in Chapter 4 of his public 2022 PhD thesis. That chapter, plus the thesis channel-model
   chapter, supplied the equation-level evidence below.
2. **P3:** this is a March 2025 arXiv preprint, not yet evidence of a peer-reviewed venue.
3. **P4:** the issue date is August 2020, although IEEE early-access publication occurred in 2019.
4. **P7:** the workbook cell combines a 2022 GLOBECOM paper and a substantially extended 2025
   *IEEE Transactions on Vehicular Technology* article. They are analyzed separately as P7a and
   P7b.
5. **P8:** the final *IEEE Access* article is from 2024, not 2023.
6. Calling an objective or reward “URLLC-aware” does not by itself make it a URLLC guarantee.
   P4 models latency and reliability violation probabilities; P5 models queueing tails; P7b uses a
   finite-blocklength rate expression; and P8 only tracks a desired SINR/data rate. These levels of
   rigor should not be conflated.

## P1 — Reinforcement learning for hybrid WiFi–VLC association

### Exact citation and access

Abdulmajeed M. Alenezi and Khairi A. Hamdi, “Reinforcement Learning Approach for Hybrid
WiFi-VLC Networks,” in *2020 IEEE 91st Vehicular Technology Conference
(VTC2020-Spring)*, Antwerp, Belgium, May 2020, pp. 1–5.

- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/VTC2020-Spring48590.2020.9128892>
- IEEE record: <https://ieeexplore.ieee.org/document/9128892>
- Author’s thesis record:
  <https://research.manchester.ac.uk/en/studentTheses/reinforcement-learning-techniques-for-next-generation-wireless-ne/>
- Public thesis full text:
  <https://pure.manchester.ac.uk/ws/portalfiles/portal/234003993/FULL_TEXT.PDF>
- **Access depth:** the closed IEEE metadata were cross-checked against the complete public thesis.
  The thesis explicitly identifies this conference paper as an output and reproduces the work in
  Chapter 4. Chapter 2 gives the channel equations used by Chapter 4. Those chapters and the
  associated simulation tables/figures were read in full.

### Research question and contribution

**Direct.** The paper asks whether centralized tabular Q-learning can improve access-point
selection in a hybrid indoor WiFi/VLC downlink. There is one WiFi AP, `N` VLC APs, and `K`
multihoming-capable users. A user can see both technologies but is single-homed for downlink data:
it must associate with exactly one AP. WiFi provides the uplink for every user. The proposed WiFi
controller learns an association that maximizes aggregate normalized downlink capacity.

The claimed novelty is a Q-learning association policy with a distance factor in its reward. The
authors say this factor favors nearby VLC APs and thereby reduces unnecessary handovers. The work
also includes stand-alone VLC layout/FoV/power studies used to motivate the hybrid system.

### Physical and MAC models

**Direct.** The VLC model is line-of-sight, static, intensity-modulated/direct-detection (IM/DD).
For transmitter `q` and user `k`, the received optical power is

`P_r = H(0) P_t`,

with DC gain

`H(0) = [A_r cos(phi) / (2 pi d_qk^2)] (m + 1) cos^m(psi)`,

where `A_r` is photodetector area, `d_qk` is link distance, `phi` and `psi` are the relevant
receiver/transmitter angles, and Lambertian order
`m = -ln(2) / ln(cos(Phi_1/2))`. Only LOS is retained. Shot and thermal noise are introduced in
the thesis background; Chapter 4 uses the aggregate optical noise PSD:

`SNR_k^VLC = (R_PD P_r)^2 / (N_0^VLC B^VLC)`.

VLC uses TDMA/round robin. Hermitian symmetry consumes half the subcarriers, giving the normalized
per-user rate at VLC AP `n`

`C_k^(VLC_n) = [1 / (2 U_n)] log2(1 + SNR_k^VLC)`,

where `U_n` is the number of users served by that lamp.

For WiFi, CSMA/CA is abstracted as a time fraction `s_k = t_k/T`, with all fractions summing to
one:

`C_k^WiFi = s_k log2(1 + SNR_k^WiFi)`.

The WiFi SNR is `|G_k,n(f)|^2 P_t / (B N_0)`. The thesis defines a channel gain from a free-space
path-loss expression

`L(d) = 20 log10(d) + 20 log10(f) - 147.5 dB`

and an i.i.d. Rayleigh small-scale term described as having 2.46 dB average power. Because there is
only one WiFi AP and WiFi and VLC occupy disjoint bands, no RF cochannel or cross-technology
interference is modeled.

The binary association variable `delta_kn` selects exactly one AP per user. The global optimization
is the sum of the WiFi and VLC user rates subject to that single-association condition.

### RL formulation

**Direct.**

- **Agent:** a centralized controller at the WiFi AP.
- **Action:** select a WiFi or VLC AP for each user. In the reported topology there are three
  choices per user: one WiFi and two VLC.
- **Exploration:** epsilon-greedy tabular Q-learning.
- **Per-user reward:**
  `R_k = a_k^WiFi C_k^WiFi + sum_n a_k^n g_k^n C_k^(VLC_n)`,
  where `g_k^n = v / d_k^(VLC_n)` and `v` is a reference distance.
- **Network reward:** `R = sum_k R_k`.
- **Update:** the standard one-step Q-learning update with learning rate `alpha` and discount
  `gamma`.
- **Stopping:** stop when the maximum Q value ceases to improve or at a fixed maximum number of
  iterations.

**Caveat.** A reproducible state definition is not supplied. The text moves directly from system
information to an AP-selection action and a Q table. It is also unclear whether one iteration
chooses a complete `K`-user joint association, updates users sequentially, or treats user actions
independently while evaluating the coupled network reward. This matters because the exact joint
action space grows as `(N+1)^K`. The thesis gives generic complexity/regret claims
`O(sqrt(S A T H^3))`, time `O(T)`, and storage `O(S A H)` with `H=1`, but these claims do not
resolve the missing state/action construction.

**Caveat.** The distance multiplier is not a handover model. All simulated users are stationary,
the state has no previous association, and neither handover count, outage, delay, nor signaling
cost is evaluated. It is therefore evidence for distance-shaped association, not evidence that
the algorithm actually controls handovers.

### Experimental design

**Direct.**

| Item | Setting |
|---|---|
| Room | `5 m x 5 m x 3 m` |
| Infrastructure | one WiFi AP and two VLC APs |
| Users | stationary, uniformly distributed |
| Trials | 40 MATLAB 2019 runs |
| VLC optical power | 25 W |
| VLC half-power angle | 60 degrees |
| Photodetector area/responsivity | 1 cm2 / 0.5 A/W |
| VLC bandwidth/noise PSD | 20 MHz / `1e-18 W/Hz` |
| WiFi carrier/bandwidth | 2.4 GHz / 20 MHz |
| WiFi noise PSD | -174 dBm/Hz |
| Q-learning maximum iterations | 60,000 |
| `epsilon`, `alpha`, `gamma` | 0.1, 0.5, 0.9 |
| Interaction granularity | one step per iteration |

Baselines are strongest-signal selection (SSS) and stand-alone WiFi. Metrics are aggregate
throughput and a minimum/worst-user throughput measure. The paper does not report packet traffic,
queues, deadline misses, reliability, or wall-clock controller latency.

The thesis also evaluates stand-alone VLC geometry. A lamp comprises 25 one-watt LEDs. It studies
two lamps in a `5 m x 5 m x 3 m` room and four lamps in an `8 m x 8 m x 3 m` room while varying
receiver FoV, lamp power/height, and AP placement.

### Quantitative findings

**Direct.**

- At nine users, the learned association is reported to raise total throughput by 182% over
  stand-alone WiFi. SSS is reported as 139% above stand-alone WiFi.
- The learned policy has the best minimum-user throughput in the plotted user-count sweep. The
  worst learned user is claimed to exceed the average SSS user.
- The Q-learning policy continues to improve after the fixed SSS assignment because its objective
  accounts for AP loading as well as channel strength.

**Direct, stand-alone VLC motivation.**

- At a 20-degree FoV, received power is roughly -13 to -15.5 dBm only directly below a lamp; at a
  70-degree FoV it spans roughly -14 to -34 dBm over a much wider region.
- Increasing lamp power from 12.5 W toward 50 W fills coverage holes but increases overlap and
  interference. At 12.5 W the illustrated SNR is around 40 dB under a lamp and around 5 dB away
  from it.
- For the 5 m room, full geometrical coverage at transmitter heights 2.15, 3.15, and 4 m requires
  an FoV of approximately 63, 55, and 50 degrees, respectively. The 8 m room at 3.15 m requires
  about 68 degrees.
- In the four-AP placement comparison, outage at a threshold around 23 dB differs by more than
  twenty percentage points: approximately 40% for one placement and above 60% for another.

**Caveat.** Results are plots/point claims without confidence intervals or seed-level dispersion.
The prose calls SSS “Algorithm 2,” although the thesis’s own algorithm numbering does not
consistently support that label.

### Assumptions and limitations

- The system is a small, static indoor downlink; there is no mobility trajectory, traffic
  process, queue, packet, URLLC deadline, or reliability model.
- Channel state and position/rate information are instantaneous and apparently perfect.
- VLC uses LOS only and WiFi uses a simple path-loss/Rayleigh model; walls, furniture, body
  blockage, orientation changes, temporal fading, optical interference outside the modeled lamps,
  and asymmetric update rates are absent.
- The WiFi and VLC schedulers are reduced to equal/time shares. Contention overhead, retransmission,
  resource-block scheduling, and control signaling are absent.
- Tabular state and joint-action definitions are incomplete, making the training result hard to
  reproduce.
- The distance reward changes the throughput objective itself; it is not a separately measured
  handover penalty or constraint.

### Concrete transfer to the planned RF/VLC/DUP simulator

**Inference / transfer.**

1. Preserve the useful structural idea: association must couple instantaneous link quality with
   current link load. A strongest-SINR baseline is insufficient when multiple flows share either
   RF or VLC resources.
2. Reuse the Lambertian LOS geometry, but extend it with vehicle LED/photodiode orientation,
   transmitter and receiver heights, field of view, road turns, inter-vehicle occlusion, ambient
   noise, and time-correlated blockage. The indoor ceiling-lamp parameters are not suitable
   V-VLC parameters.
3. Do not transplant `v/d` as a handover mechanism. Include previous mode/link in the state and
   model switching interruption, signaling/processing time, packet continuity, and explicit
   switch cost. Then measure handover rate and deadline misses.
4. Make the controller’s action semantics explicit. For one transmitting vehicle, an action such
   as `{RF, VLC, DUP}` is tractable. If many transmitter–receiver pairs are controlled jointly,
   use factorization or multi-agent control rather than an unenumerated exponential table.
5. Use SSS and standalone-RF as simple sanity baselines, but also add queue-aware and
   reliability-aware heuristics. Throughput alone cannot validate a URLLC controller.

## P2 — Context-aware Q-learning network selection

### Exact citation and access

Chunxi Wang, Guofeng Wu, Zhiyong Du, and Bin Jiang, “Reinforcement Learning Based Network
Selection for Hybrid VLC and RF Systems,” *MATEC Web of Conferences*, vol. 173, article 03014,
2018.

- DOI and open article: <https://doi.org/10.1051/matecconf/201817303014>
- Full-text PDF:
  <https://www.matec-conferences.org/10.1051/matecconf/201817303014/pdf>
- **Access depth:** complete six-page publisher PDF read.

### Problem and contribution

**Direct.** A multihoming terminal is in overlapping LTE, WLAN, and VLC coverage but chooses one
network for each 30-second decision interval. The best network depends on whether traffic is
uplink-dominant, downlink-dominant, or symmetric, on which networks are available, and on a
time-of-day/context period. The proposed solution is tabular Q-learning augmented by:

1. pruning actions that are unavailable in the current location/context; and
2. transferring/reusing a Q table learned in a similar context instead of cold-starting.

The contribution is therefore context-aware selection and faster learning, not a new RF/VLC
physical layer.

### Network and utility models

**Direct.**

- LTE downlink uses a weighted proportional-fair rate abstraction; LTE uplink receives a fair
  share.
- WLAN throughput is abstracted through a DCF/harmonic-share formulation.
- VLC downlink uses TDMA/round robin, with a `1/(2K)` factor for Hermitian symmetry and user
  sharing. The uplink is infrared rather than visible light.
- Only one network is selected per epoch; there is no packet duplication or traffic splitting.

The paper maps rates to traffic-dependent utility:

- uplink-dominant traffic rewards adequate uplink and uses a threshold/log-type form;
- downlink-dominant traffic similarly prioritizes downlink;
- symmetric traffic is limited by the smaller of uplink and downlink utility.

The objective is expected discounted cumulative utility, not raw throughput.

### RL formulation

**Direct.**

- **State/context:** traffic class, current set of available networks, and time-period context.
- **Action:** choose one of LTE, WLAN, or VLC, restricted to currently available options.
- **Reward:** the traffic-class utility resulting from observed uplink/downlink rates.
- **Algorithm:** epsilon-greedy tabular Q-learning.
- **Knowledge transfer:** initialize a new context from a stored Q table judged similar to it.

The paper compares random selection, ordinary Q-learning, action-reduction only, Q-table reuse only,
and the complete method.

### Experimental settings and findings

**Direct.** The simulated overlap contains one LTE femtocell, one WLAN AP, and one VLC AP, each
with at most six users. Rather than a geometry/channel simulator, network rates are discretized
from Speedtest-like ranges. Settings include a 30 s selection period, at most 500 Monte Carlo
iterations, learning rate 0.3, discount 0.3, cold-start exploration 0.6, transferred-context
exploration 0.4, and utility parameters reported as `lambda=2`, `beta=3`, `c=10`,
`theta_1=200 kb/s`, and `theta_2=5000 kb/s`.

The complete reduced-action/transfer method reaches its stable utility in fewer than about 50
iterations in the plotted cases. Plain cold-start Q-learning is slower, while each of the two
augmentations alone supplies only part of the gain. The figures support the convergence ordering
but do not provide a robust exact percentage improvement.

**Caveat.** The listed VLC uplink/downlink numeric ranges appear reversed relative to the paper’s
own narrative: the table prints a much larger VLC “uplink” range (roughly 8,000–13,000 kb/s) than
its “downlink” range (roughly 80–120 kb/s), despite describing high-rate visible-light downlink and
IR uplink. These numbers should not seed a simulator without checking the authors’ original code,
which is not supplied.

### Assumptions and limitations

- Location is reduced to an available-network set; no continuous mobility, dwell time, blockage,
  angle, fading, interference, or handover interruption is simulated.
- Rate ranges replace physical RF/VLC channel models.
- There are no packet arrivals, queues, deadlines, outage events, retransmissions, or reliability
  constraints.
- Q-table similarity and the mechanism that chooses a source context are underspecified.
- A 30-second control epoch is several orders of magnitude too slow for vehicular URLLC link
  selection.
- The method selects one network and cannot express duplicated transmission.

### Concrete transfer to the planned RF/VLC/DUP simulator

**Inference / transfer.**

1. Retain traffic/service class and link availability as state features. A controller can
   reasonably make different RF/VLC/DUP choices for safety packets, sensor sharing, and
   best-effort traffic.
2. Treat action masking as an important implementation technique: mask VLC when LOS/FoV or
   hardware conditions make it impossible, and mask DUP if either leg lacks a schedulable
   resource. Record masking so policy comparisons remain fair.
3. Warm-starting across related road segments, traffic densities, or weather conditions is a
   useful later experiment, but it needs an explicit context-distance definition and a
   from-scratch control.
4. Do not reuse the paper’s rate table or time scale. The planned simulator needs slot/packet-level
   NR and VLC dynamics, physical mobility, queues, and deadline checks.

## P3 — Sequential PPO user association in hybrid LiFi/WiFi

### Exact citation and access

Peijun Hou and Nan Cen, “Deep Reinforcement Learning-Based User Association in Hybrid LiFi/WiFi
Indoor Networks,” arXiv:2503.01803, submitted 3 March 2025.

- arXiv record: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2503.01803>
- Full-text PDF: <https://arxiv.org/pdf/2503.01803>
- arXiv record DOI: <https://doi.org/10.48550/arXiv.2503.01803>
- **Access depth:** complete arXiv full text read.
- **Publication status:** preprint. The arXiv DOI identifies the repository record, not a
  peer-reviewed publication.

### Research question and contribution

**Direct.** The paper addresses combinatorial downlink user association in an indoor hybrid
LiFi/WiFi network with capacity-limited APs, time-varying channels, mobility, and stochastic LiFi
blockage. It proposes “sequential PPO” (S-PPO): instead of selecting a joint association from
`(L+W)^K` possibilities, a centralized actor assigns users one at a time, reducing each decision
to `K(L+W)` categorical choices while preserving a global network reward.

The stated objective is per-slot aggregate throughput. The work is not queue-aware, and it does
not model link-switching delay, handover cost, reliability targets, or deadline-constrained
traffic.

### Physical and mobility models

**Direct.** The room is `10 m x 10 m x 3.5 m`. Users are single-homed and APs use TDMA, so a
selected AP’s capacity/resource is shared among its associated users.

For a LOS LiFi AP, the optical DC gain has the usual Lambertian form:

`H = [(m+1) A_PD / (2 pi d^2)] cos^m(phi) T_s(phi) g(phi) cos(psi)`

inside the receiver FoV and zero outside it. Optical SINR squares the desired electrical signal
and includes squared contributions from the other LiFi APs. The paper uses the IM/DD lower bound

`R_LiFi = (B_L/2) log2[1 + (e/(2 pi)) gamma_L]`.

The WiFi channel uses free-space path loss plus zero-mean Gaussian shadowing. Shadowing standard
deviation changes from 3 dB to 5 dB at a 5 m link distance. Small-scale fading is Rician with
`K=1` within 5 m and Rayleigh (`K=0`) farther away. WiFi rate is represented as
`(B_W/2) log2(1+gamma_W)`. There is no modeled WiFi cochannel interference.

Users follow random waypoint mobility at 0.5–2 m/s. Channels are sampled every 0.1 s. A blockage
event is independently sampled for each user/link/time slot at a configured rate.

**Caveat.** The blockage process is memoryless and the attenuation/mechanism applied during a
blocked sample is not specified clearly enough for exact reproduction. It does not represent the
duration, spatial footprint, or common-cause correlation of a person/vehicle occluder.

### RL formulation

**Direct.**

- **Agent:** one centralized controller.
- **Observation:** global per-user SINRs toward all LiFi and WiFi APs, processed in a sequential
  user-assignment procedure.
- **Action:** choose one AP for the current user.
- **Reward:** aggregate system throughput after the association, with a large soft penalty when
  an AP’s capacity constraint is violated.
- **Actor:** a categorical stochastic policy.
- **Critic:** estimates the state value; critic loss is mean squared error.
- **Training:** clipped PPO surrogate objective.

Reported hyperparameters include actor/critic learning rate `1e-4`, clip ratio 0.1, discount 0.99,
eight optimization epochs per update, and an update interval of 450 interactions. The paper says
both networks have two hidden layers but does not supply widths, activations, batch size, GAE
parameter, entropy/value coefficients, normalization, or random seeds.

### Experimental design

**Direct.**

| Item | LiFi | WiFi |
|---|---:|---:|
| Bandwidth | 40 MHz | 10 MHz |
| Transmit power | 3 W optical | 0.1 W |
| Noise PSD | `1e-21` | `4.002e-17` |
| Half-power angle | 60 degrees | n/a |
| PD area/FoV/responsivity | 1 cm2 / 90 degrees / 0.53 A/W | n/a |
| Refractive index | 1.5 | n/a |

The vertical LiFi separation is varied in the 1.5–2 m range. Tests include static and mobile
users, “free” and “prone”/blocked conditions, and denser layouts. Baselines are exhaustive search
where tractable, strongest-signal selection (SSS), and TRPO.

### Quantitative findings

**Direct.**

- In tractable static cases, S-PPO is reported to reach 100% of exhaustive search’s throughput.
- Depending on blockage/scenario, its throughput is as much as 35.5% above SSS.
- In static free and blockage-prone settings, convergence is reported as approximately three and
  six times faster than TRPO, respectively.
- For mobile users, the paper reports up to a 300% improvement in one comparison and states in the
  conclusion that convergence is 267% faster than TRPO. Those differently worded headline values
  should not be merged into one precise statistic.
- Averaged over the reported scenarios, S-PPO is stated to improve throughput/fairness over SSS by
  32.25%/19.09%, and over TRPO by 10.34%/10.23%.
- With six LiFi APs in a denser case, S-PPO reaches 92.2% of exhaustive search performance.
- A reported 18-user timing comparison gives 303.3 s training and 0.000532 s inference for S-PPO,
  versus 322.8 s and 0.000589 s for TRPO.

**Caveat.** The paper has no uncertainty intervals, seed accounting, or deployment-hardware
description for the timing numbers. “Fairness” is reported in the results but the optimization
reward is aggregate throughput, so fairness is an outcome rather than an enforced objective.

### Assumptions and limitations

- It is an indoor ceiling-lamp downlink, not V2V or V2I. Pedestrian random-waypoint mobility does
  not exercise road topology, lane changes, turns, occluding vehicles, or relative orientation.
- There are no packets, arrivals, queues, finite blocklength, retransmissions, deadlines, or
  reliability/outage constraints.
- Association changes have no interruption or signaling cost, so the policy may switch freely.
- The capacity constraint is a reward penalty, not a hard-feasible action construction.
- Important PPO/network/reproducibility settings are missing.
- A centralized all-user SINR tensor assumes fresh global measurements and ignores observation
  delay/overhead.

### Concrete transfer to the planned RF/VLC/DUP simulator

**Inference / transfer.**

1. Sequential action factorization is useful if the eventual controller serves many V2V pairs.
   It should be benchmarked against a per-pair decentralized or parameter-shared policy because
   the ordering of sequential assignments can introduce bias.
2. Preserve the physical LiFi lesson that desired and interfering optical amplitudes enter the
   electrical SINR differently from RF power. Replace the ceiling geometry and independent
   blockage with headlamp/taillamp radiation, receiver orientation, road-line LOS, weather, and
   correlated vehicle occlusion.
3. Include previous association, queue ages, and feature ages; a pure instantaneous-SINR
   observation cannot learn handover hysteresis or packet urgency.
4. Use S-PPO as a possible unconstrained throughput baseline, not as the main CMDP solution.
   Deadline-miss cost and RF resource/interference cost must remain explicit.

## P4 — Energy-efficient RF/VLC resource management for industrial URLLC

### Exact citation and access

Helin Yang, Arokiaswami Alphones, Wen-De Zhong, Chen Chen, and Xianzhong Xie, “Learning-Based
Energy-Efficient Resource Management by Heterogeneous RF/VLC for Ultra-Reliable Low-Latency
Industrial IoT Networks,” *IEEE Transactions on Industrial Informatics*, vol. 16, no. 8,
pp. 5565–5576, August 2020.

- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/TII.2019.2933867>
- NTU repository record: <https://hdl.handle.net/10356/142892>
- Public accepted manuscript:
  <https://dr.ntu.edu.sg/server/api/core/bitstreams/440e0ef9-97a3-4cac-a518-791f5c4eb990/content>
- **Access depth:** complete accepted manuscript read. Its front matter is the 2019 IEEE
  early-access version; citation metadata were cross-checked against the final 2020 issue.

### Research question and contribution

**Direct.** The paper jointly chooses network, subchannel, and power for a heterogeneous indoor
industrial RF/VLC network. Half the devices carry URLLC-like industrial traffic, while half carry
normal high-rate traffic. The objective is network energy efficiency—total data rate divided by
total power—while penalizing unsatisfied latency, reliability, and minimum-rate requirements.

Its algorithmic contribution is deep post-decision-state experience replay and transfer
(PDS-ERT). A deep Q network uses post-decision information and transfers samples/actions from
similar historical agents to accelerate learning in a large state/action problem.

### Architecture and channel models

**Direct.**

- A `24 m x 24 m x 6 m` factory contains a centered RF AP and a `6 x 6` ceiling grid of VLC APs
  at height 5 m.
- Every device has RF and VLC receivers. VLC is used for downlink; RF supports downlink and
  uplink.
- Devices are randomly placed at heights 0.5, 1, 1.5, and 2 m.
- Both technologies use OFDMA/subchannels. Interference from adjacent rooms/cells is modeled.

The VLC channel uses a Lambertian LOS DC gain with FoV filtering. Its downlink SINR includes the
desired AP, cochannel VLC interference, and electrical noise; achievable rate includes the
`1/2` IM/DD/Hermitian factor.

The RF channel gain is `10^(-PL/10)`, with industrial path loss

`PL[dB] = A log10(d) + B + E log10(f_c/5) + X`.

For LOS, `(A,B,E)=(18.7,46.8,20)`; for NLOS, `(36.8,43.8,20)`. Thin-wall/obstacle loss is
`X = 5(N_wall-1)`. RF downlink and uplink SINR include cochannel interference and thermal noise.

Total power includes RF/VLC fixed AP consumption, VLC electrical transmit power, RF AP/device
transmit power, and device circuit power. The VLC fixed term is said to include illumination
power.

### Traffic, latency, and reliability treatment

**Direct.** URLLC device arrivals are i.i.d. Poisson with rate `lambda` and fixed packet size
`L_packet`. The end-to-end latency decomposition is

`T_l = T_w + T_t + T_a + T_b + T_r + T_p`,

where waiting, transmission, access, backhaul, reception, and processing components are named
explicitly and `T_t=L_packet/R_d`.

The paper defines latency violation

`p_k^Lat = Pr(T_l >= T_max) <= p_max^Lat`

and reliability as an SINR outage probability

`p_k^Rel = Pr(gamma_k,n < gamma_k,n^tar) <= p_max^Rel`.

Normal-service dissatisfaction is

`p_k^Nor = Pr(R_k < R_k^min) <= p_max^Nor`.

The actual scalar reward is

`r = eta_EE - mu_1 sum_k p_k^Lat - mu_2 sum_k p_k^Rel - mu_3 sum_k p_k^Nor`.

**Caveat.** Although the paper first writes these as constraints, the learned problem implements
them through fixed soft penalties. It does not use a Lagrange multiplier, project onto a feasible
set, prove a chance constraint, or report confidence bounds on rare-event probabilities.

### RL formulation and PDS-ERT mechanism

**Direct.**

- **Agents:** RF AP, VLC APs, and active devices are described collectively as agents.
- **State:** subchannel idle/busy status, channel quality/SINR, service class
  (normal or URLLC), and satisfaction of reliability/latency/minimum-rate requirements.
- **Action:** RF/VLC AP selection, subchannel assignment, and transmit-power management.
- **Reward:** the energy-efficiency/violation expression above.
- **Discounted control:** DQN-based value learning with discount `xi`.

A transition is split into a known immediate component from state/action to a post-decision state
and an unknown environmental component from that post-decision state to the next state. PDS-ERT
stores these transitions, searches historical agents/experiences for similarity using a Bregman
divergence/“Bregman ball,” and combines a current native action with transferred expert action
information. The paper provides a convergence theorem under generic stochastic-approximation
conditions.

**Caveat.** Network selection and subchannel assignment are discrete. The described weighted
mixing of a native action and historical/expert action is straightforward for continuous power
but ambiguous for categorical AP/channel indices unless an unreported encoding/projection is
used. The system also alternates between centralized and multi-agent language without a fully
specified joint training/execution architecture.

Average latency near 1 ms does not establish that the probability of exceeding 1 ms is acceptably

### Assumptions and limitations

- Devices are static random indoor points; there is no mobility, road geometry, handover, V2V,
  sidelink, or vehicular VLC.
- Arrivals are memoryless Poisson and packet size is fixed. Queue discipline, finite buffers,
  drops, retransmissions, and coding blocklength are not fully specified.
- Optical blockage is an independent scalar probability, not geometric/time-correlated
  obstruction.
- Reliability is an SINR threshold event, not decoded packet success by a deadline.
- Fixed penalty coefficients can trade constraint violations for energy efficiency and require
  scenario-specific tuning.
- Exact action discretization, DQN output encoding, replay composition, transferred-action
  projection, training seeds, and target-network details are insufficient for faithful
  reproduction.

### Concrete transfer to the planned RF/VLC/DUP simulator

**Inference / transfer.**

1. P4 provides the strongest decomposition in this batch for a first packet-latency budget:
   queueing + transmission + access/scheduling + processing. Use explicit timestamps and measure
   the empirical event `delivery_time > deadline`, rather than optimizing mean delay alone.
2. Keep service class, queue state, channel/load state, and satisfaction history in the
   observation. Avoid handing the controller an oracle “future satisfaction” variable.
3. Separate reward from constraint cost. A natural CMDP has utility/resource reward and
   per-packet deadline-miss cost, with a learned Lagrange multiplier and reported confidence
   interval for violation rate. P4’s fixed-penalty reward is a valuable ablation.
4. The RF/VLC energy accounting is a useful template, but DUP must charge both legs’ scheduled
   resources and energy. Illumination power should be excluded or treated as sunk energy for
   vehicle lamps if they would be on regardless of communications.
5. Do not copy the indoor RF path-loss or VLC ceiling layout into Manhattan V2V. Use NR sidelink
   and road/building/vehicle geometry, while preserving the idea that RF and VLC can have
   complementary blockage/interference patterns.

## P5 — Hybrid RF/VLC link selection under statistical queueing constraints

### Exact citation and access

Marwan Hammouda, Sami Akin, Anna Maria Vegni, Harald Haas, and Jürgen Peissig, “Link Selection
in Hybrid RF/VLC Systems Under Statistical Queueing Constraints,” *IEEE Transactions on
Wireless Communications*, vol. 17, no. 4, pp. 2738–2754, April 2018.

- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/TWC.2018.2802937>
- arXiv record: <https://arxiv.org/abs/1711.08175>
- Full-text PDF: <https://arxiv.org/pdf/1711.08175>
- **Access depth:** complete 29-page author preprint read and bibliographic details checked
  against the final journal record.

### Research question and contribution

**Direct.** The paper asks how much Markov-modulated traffic a hybrid RF/VLC transmitter can
sustain while satisfying statistical buffer-overflow/queueing-delay constraints, and how RF,
VLC, link selection, simultaneous transmission, and handover delay compare.

Its contribution is analytical rather than RL-based:

1. derive maximum sustainable average arrival rates using effective-capacity/log-MGF methods;
2. select RF or VLC according to the resulting queue-constrained service support;
3. analyze simultaneous RF+VLC transmission with optimized power division;
4. incorporate link-switching delay through a finite-state Markov chain; and
5. derive non-asymptotic stochastic-network-calculus backlog and delay bounds.

This paper is important because it makes traffic/service variability and queue tails first-class,
unlike throughput-only association papers.

### Channel and traffic models

**Direct.** Time is divided into frames of duration `T`. A transmitter with an infinite buffer
communicates to a receiver through one RF AP/link and one VLC AP/link under an average power
constraint.

The RF service is a random block-fading process. The channel is constant within a frame and i.i.d.
across frames, with Rician fading, lognormal shadowing, distance path loss, and receiver thermal
noise. A truncated-Gaussian-input capacity expression/upper bound is used under average and peak
power constraints.

The VLC link is static LOS IM/DD. With parallel transmitter/receiver planes its gain is

`g = [(s+1) A u(psi) d_v^(s+1)] / [2 pi d_1^(s+3)] rect(psi/psi_C)`,

where `s` is Lambertian order, `A` is PD area, `u` concentrator gain, `d_v` vertical separation,
`d_1` link distance, and `psi_C` FoV. The paper uses optical-capacity lower bounds compatible with
nonnegative intensity and peak/average illumination constraints. Its per-frame VLC service is
constant for a fixed position and allocation.

Arrivals follow a two-state ON/OFF Markov chain. In ON, `lambda` bits arrive per frame; in OFF,
zero arrive. If the OFF-to-ON probability is `beta` and ON-to-OFF is `alpha`, stationary mean
arrival is

`rho_bar = [beta/(alpha+beta)] lambda`.

The log moment-generating functions of the arrival and service processes are matched to obtain a
sustainable arrival under a QoS exponent `theta`.

### Statistical queueing framework

**Direct.** For stationary queue backlog `Q`,

`Pr(Q >= q) approximately exp(-theta q)`

in the asymptotic effective-capacity interpretation. Larger `theta` means a faster-decaying tail
and hence a more stringent statistical QoS requirement. The maximum sustainable arrival is found
where the arrival effective bandwidth and service effective capacity balance. The paper then uses
stochastic network calculus to give finite/non-asymptotic upper bounds for backlog and buffering
delay violation probabilities.

**Caveat.** Neither an effective-capacity exponent nor an SNC upper bound is the same as an
empirically calibrated packet deadline-miss probability. Bounds may be conservative, and the
asymptotic approximation depends on stationarity and regularity assumptions.

### Link strategies

**Direct.**

- **RF-only** and **VLC-only** provide reference service processes.
- **Type I hybrid:** in every frame select whichever link has the greater instantaneous service
  rate. Because VLC rate is static while RF fades, this becomes an RF-channel threshold rule.
- **Type II hybrid:** send independent data on RF and VLC simultaneously. Divide the total power
  to maximize the supported queue-constrained arrival rate.
- **Handover-delay hybrid:** represent each frame as `n` subframes. A `2n+2`-state discrete-time
  Markov chain tracks RF subframes, VLC subframes, and the two handover directions. Its
  log-spectral radius determines effective capacity with lost handover service.

Perfect instantaneous RF CSI is assumed for selection and supplied to the transmitter through
delay-free, error-free feedback. A fast, stable RF/VLC handover mechanism is assumed.

### Experimental settings

**Direct.**

| Item | Setting |
|---|---|
| Frame duration | `1e-4 s` |
| VLC half-intensity angles | 30, 45, 60 degrees |
| VLC PD area/FoV/concentrator | 1 cm2 / 90 degrees / 1 |
| VLC responsivity | 0.53 A/W |
| VLC vertical distance | 2.5 m |
| VLC bandwidth/noise PSD | 10 MHz / `1e-21 A2/Hz` |
| RF bandwidth | 10 MHz |
| RF path-loss exponent | 1.8 |
| RF Rician factor | 10 dB |
| RF lognormal standard deviation | 3.6 dB |
| RF ambient temperature | 280 K |
| RF reference loss | 40 dB at 1 m, 2.4 GHz |

Representative plots place the receiver 3 m from VLC and 15 m from RF, use
`alpha=0.3`, `beta=0.7`, and sweep average power, peak/average ratio
`nu=P_avg/P_peak`, QoS exponent, user count, position, and handover duration.

### Quantitative findings

**Direct.**

- With `nu=0.3` and `theta=0.01`, increasing average power from 27 to 28 dBm raises
  VLC-supported arrival by more than 145 bits/frame but RF-supported arrival by only about
  2 bits/frame. The static VLC service benefits sharply from optical power; RF tail service is
  limited by fading.
- RF is stronger at low power/loose QoS. VLC overtakes it at higher power or stricter `theta`;
  the RF-supported rate tends toward zero much faster as `theta` grows.
- When resources are shared uniformly, RF’s per-user supported rate is generally higher than
  VLC’s above roughly four served receivers in the illustrated configuration.
- Type I and Type II hybrid schemes expand the spatial/arrival-rate region beyond either
  stand-alone link. Type II also benefits from simultaneous service but consumes both resources.
- Handover delay materially lowers sustainable arrival; increasing subframes `n` (shortening the
  relative handover phase) approaches the ideal no-delay curve.
- SNC plots show that hybrid service can support a wider range of arrivals with lower delay bounds
  than RF-only, while VLC-only can have the smallest bounds only over its smaller admissible
  arrival range.

**Caveat.** Most hybrid gains are curves without tabulated numerical deltas or uncertainty. They
are analytical/numerical evaluations under the assumed distributions, not measurements from a
packet simulator or testbed.

### Assumptions and limitations

- The receiver position and VLC channel are static. “Mobility” enters primarily through link
  availability/selection and an abstract handover penalty, not a trajectory.
- Perfect CSI and zero-cost feedback are assumed.
- RF frames are independent; shared interference and correlated blockage/fading are absent.
- The buffer is infinite, transmission errors/decoding failures are not modeled explicitly, and
  there is no finite-blocklength reliability model.
- ON/OFF Markov arrivals are useful but much simpler than event-triggered/cooperative-driving
  traffic.
- The analytical simultaneous-link strategy sends independent data, not duplicate copies of the
  same packet. Its reliability/latency semantics therefore differ from DUP.
- Statistical delay bounds do not identify individual packet deadlines or account for stale
  observations/controller computation.

### Concrete transfer to the planned RF/VLC/DUP simulator

**Inference / transfer.**

1. Use P5 as the queueing baseline for validating the simulator. In simplified stationary
   ON/OFF cases, empirical backlog and delay tails should move in the predicted direction as
   `theta`, power, and handover time vary.
2. Include at least one bursty Markov arrival generator in addition to Poisson traffic. State
   should include queue length, head-of-line age/slack, and perhaps recent arrival-rate features.
3. Implement handover/switching as lost time or unavailable service, not only a scalar negative
   reward. That makes its effect propagate naturally into queue and deadline outcomes.
4. Distinguish traffic splitting from packet duplication. For DUP, the same packet occupies RF
   and VLC resources and completes at the first successful arrival; the slower copy may require
   cancellation semantics.
5. Use effective-capacity/SNC policies as analytical or heuristic baselines, while reporting
   direct Monte Carlo deadline-miss estimates and confidence intervals for the CMDP constraint.

## P6 — Optimal CTMDP handover in vehicular VLC/RF

### Exact citation and access

Linqiong Jia, Shicheng Feng, Yijin Zhang, and Jin-Yuan Wang, “A Hybrid Handover Scheme for
Vehicular VLC/RF Communication Networks,” *Sensors*, vol. 24, no. 13, article 4323, 3 July 2024.

- DOI and open publisher article: <https://doi.org/10.3390/s24134323>
- PubMed Central full text: <https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11244001/>
- **Access depth:** complete open-access publisher/PMC article read.

### Research question and contribution

**Direct.** The paper designs an “optimal handover” (O-HO) policy for a vehicle moving through
streetlight VLC cells while cellular RF remains continuously available. VLC is downlink; RF is
used for uplink and as fallback downlink. The vehicle has a finite FIFO buffer. The controller
must balance packet loss/queueing delay, RF/VLC transmission energy, vertical handover cost, and
the risk of staying too long on a degrading/blocked VLC link.

The authors formulate the choice as a continuous-time Markov decision process (CTMDP), uniformize
it, and solve discounted value iteration. This is dynamic programming with known rates, not
model-free reinforcement learning.

### Vehicular architecture

**Direct.**

- Streetlights are VLC APs connected to a cellular base station.
- Adjacent VLC APs use alternating frequency bands to reduce optical cochannel interference.
- Neighboring VLC cells overlap, permitting soft horizontal handover.
- RF is always available, and the terminal has an RF uplink.
- If current VLC becomes blocked, the vehicle may wait, vertically hand over to RF, or hand over
  early to the next VLC AP when overlap/visibility permits.

The paper abstracts physical channels into ON/OFF service availability and Poisson service rates.
It therefore captures stochastic link availability and overlap progression but not optical
received power, FoV, weather, orientation, NR resource scheduling, or explicit interferer
geometry.

### Queueing CTMDP

**Direct.** The state is

`x = (s, s', h, b, w)`,

where `s` and `s'` are current/next VLC link states (ON/OFF), `h` indicates whether the vehicle is
inside an overlap region, `b` is queue occupancy from 0 through finite capacity `B`, and `w`
identifies the currently used technology/mode (RF or VLC).

Arrivals are Poisson with rate `lambda`; RF and VLC service completions are Poisson with rates
`mu_RF` and `mu_VLC`. VLC ON/OFF transitions use rates `gamma` and `beta`, and road position moves
between non-overlap/overlap phases with rates `alpha_12` and `alpha_21`.

The action encoding is:

- `-1`: connect/hand over to the next VLC AP;
- `0`: use RF;
- `1`: retain/use the current VLC AP.

The continuous-time process is uniformized into an equivalent discrete-time MDP. Discounted value
iteration finds the stationary action minimizing expected cumulative cost.

### Cost model

**Direct.** Per-state/action cost combines:

- RF/VLC transmission energy;
- a queue-occupancy term `zeta b`;
- vertical-handover and horizontal-handover signaling energy;
- packet-loss penalty when the finite buffer overflows; and
- an additional delay-related/handover term proportional to `theta lambda tau`.

Vertical handover is assigned a much larger signaling cost than horizontal VLC-to-VLC handover.
This makes early HHO attractive when the next VLC AP is ready, but RF attractive when waiting
would increase loss/delay.

### Experimental settings

**Direct.**

| Item | Setting |
|---|---:|
| Arrival rate `lambda` | 2 |
| VLC/RF service rates | 3.8 / 2.2 |
| VLC availability rates | `gamma=0.4`, `beta` swept 0.3–1.1 |
| Mobility-region rates | `alpha_21=0.4`, `alpha_12=0.2` |
| RF/VLC transmission energy | `5e-4 kWh` each in the parameter table |
| HHO/VHO delay | 0.1 / 0.1–0.5 |
| VHO/HHO signaling cost | 418 J / 0 J |
| Buffer capacity | 10 packets |
| Packet-loss penalty `theta` | 1000 J/packet |
| Queue weight `zeta` | 500 J |
| Dwell thresholds in baseline | 0.3 and 0.6 |
| Simulation duration | 40,000 s |
| Discount / value tolerance | 0.99 / `1e-5` |
| Two cost weights | `xi_1=1`, `xi_2=0.75` |

Baselines are immediate handover in the full hybrid system, dwell-time hybrid handover, immediate
vertical handover, and dwell-time vertical handover. Metrics include loss probability, mean
delay, average queue, and vertical-handover count.

### Quantitative findings

**Direct/graph reading.**

- O-HO stays close to immediate-handover loss, delay, and queue performance while reducing VHO
  count to roughly two-thirds or below one-half of immediate handover, depending on the sweep.
- Immediate hybrid/VHO and O-HO generally have delay and queue values around half those of the
  more conservative dwell policies in adverse settings.
- As VHO delay rises, dwell schemes become roughly 1.5–2 times worse in delay-related metrics,
  while O-HO remains near immediate handover.
- O-HO’s VHO count is often close to that of the 0.6 dwell policy while preserving the lower
  queue/loss behavior of immediate switching.

**Caveat.** These are approximate visual comparisons; the article supplies plots rather than raw
tables, seed-level results, or confidence intervals.

### Assumptions and limitations

- Channel geometry is compressed to exponential ON/OFF and location-region transitions.
  VLC/RF rates are known, stationary Poisson service parameters.
- There is no NR sidelink, physical RF/VLC SINR, finite blocklength, interference, packet decoding,
  retransmission, weather, turning, or vehicle-to-vehicle orientation.
- Exponential dwell/service assumptions are memoryless; real road/occlusion durations are
  time-correlated and often predictable from geometry.
- The 40,000 s aggregate horizon and long-scale handover process are not evidence of sub-ms URLLC
  behavior.
- RF is assumed continuously available, and horizontal VLC handover is assigned zero signaling
  energy in the main parameters.
- Optimization relies on a known transition generator; online model estimation/adaptation is not
  addressed.

### Concrete transfer to the planned RF/VLC/DUP simulator

**Inference / transfer.**

1. P6 supplies an excellent small-state oracle. Build a reduced ON/OFF/finite-buffer version of
   the simulator and compare learned policies with CTMDP value iteration before scaling to the
   Manhattan physical model.
2. Include the next-link/near-future geometrical context when causally observable: distance to the
   next turn or lamp/vehicle, LOS forecast, overlap, and time-to-exit. This is more useful than
   purely reactive SINR.
3. Put current mode, previous action, and time since switch in the state. Charge real switching
   interruption and signaling/resource costs so hysteresis emerges from dynamics.
4. Expand the action set from exclusive `{RF,VLC}` to `{RF,VLC,DUP}`. DUP is not just another
   handover action: it consumes two services concurrently and has first-success completion.
5. Retain a finite buffer and explicit drop reason. Separate buffer overflow, deadline expiry, link
   failure, and action infeasibility in logs.

## P7a — Joint V2I network selection and autonomous driving

### Exact citation and access

Zijiang Yan and Hina Tabassum, “Reinforcement Learning for Joint V2I Network Selection and
Autonomous Driving Policies,” in *2022 IEEE Global Communications Conference (GLOBECOM)*,
Rio de Janeiro, Brazil, December 2022, pp. 1241–1246.

- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/GLOBECOM48099.2022.10001396>
- arXiv record: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2208.02249>
- Full-text PDF: <https://arxiv.org/pdf/2208.02249>
- **Access depth:** complete author preprint read and citation checked against IEEE metadata.

### Research question and contribution

**Direct.** The paper jointly controls an autonomous vehicle’s longitudinal/lateral driving action
and its downlink V2I association to a radio base station (RBS) or terahertz base station (TBS).
Fast THz links are short-range and handover-prone; sub-6 GHz links are slower but more robust.
Driving decisions change geometry and therefore future communication quality, while network
selection affects rate and handover.

The work compares tabular Q-learning with DQN on a scalar reward that sums transport and
telecommunication terms. It is one of the few papers in this batch to couple mobility control and
network control, but it does not model VLC, packet queues, URLLC deadlines, or duplication.

### Road, RF, and THz models

**Direct.** Autonomous vehicles travel on a two-lane road under a two-tier downlink network:
sub-6 GHz RBSs and 1 THz TBSs. Each vehicle associates with one BS.

RBS channels use distance path loss, Rayleigh fading, and cochannel interference. For the THz
link, desired received power includes:

- free-space spreading loss;
- frequency/distance-dependent molecular absorption;
- highly directional antenna gain; and
- THz molecular/noise and interference terms.

For either tier, data rate is Shannon-like:

`R = W log2(1 + SINR)`.

Each BS has a finite user quota. The network quality heuristic used by the joint action is

`T_q = [data rate / min(quota, current load)] (1 - mu)`,

where the mobility/technology penalty `mu` is 0.5 for a new TBS, 0.1 for a new RBS, and 0 when
staying with the current BS. The reward further reduces this value as cumulative handover
frequency grows.

**Caveat.** Dividing by `min(quota,load)` is a coarse equal-share approximation. The formulation
does not model PRBs, queues, scheduling, retransmission, control overhead, or handover
interruption.

### MDP and joint actions

**Direct.** The vehicle state has eight quantities describing distances and relative velocities to
nearby vehicles, the number of the three best BSs that can meet a rate threshold, and lane
identity. Continuous quantities are discretized for tabular Q-learning.

There are seven driving choices:

1. hard acceleration;
2. mild acceleration;
3. maintain speed;
4. mild deceleration;
5. hard deceleration;
6. lane change; and
7. stop.

Each is paired with one of three network policies:

1. choose the BS maximizing the handover/load-adjusted `T_q`;
2. use a no-handover variant that stays with the current BS; or
3. choose maximum instantaneous rate.

The combined action space therefore has 21 actions.

Transport reward penalizes collision, undesired speed, unsafe spacing, acceleration/deceleration,
and lane change. Telecom reward is proportional to adjusted throughput and penalized by
cumulative handover frequency. The final reward is their weighted scalar sum.

The DQN uses replay and a target network updated every 50 episodes. The architecture is described
as ReLU, sigmoid, and linear layers, with epsilon annealing.

**Caveat.** The method reports binary cross-entropy loss for Q-value regression. BCE is normally
for probabilities/binary labels, whereas unconstrained Q targets can be negative and unbounded.
Unless the omitted implementation transforms targets, this is a methodological/reproducibility
concern.

### Experimental settings

**Direct.** The default deployment has four 3.5 GHz RBSs and ten 1 THz TBSs. RBS/TBS user quotas
are 2/5. Representative physical parameters include 40 MHz RF bandwidth, 500 MHz THz bandwidth,
1 W power, 316.2 directional THz gain, absorption coefficient 0.05, and path-loss exponent 4.
Reward magnitudes heavily prioritize collisions: its collision weight is 1000, desired-speed
weight is 5, and most other transport weights are 1. The telecom scale is swept roughly from
`4.5e-6` to `6.5e-6`.

Baselines include nearest/minimum-distance BS association, tabular Q-learning, and action-policy
variants. Plots vary desired speed, TBS density, autonomous-vehicle density, and reward balance.

### Findings

**Direct/qualitative.**

- DQN obtains the best plotted aggregate reward and learns safer/more communication-aware actions
  than tabular Q-learning and minimum-distance selection.
- A desired-speed range around 40–50 in the paper’s units produces the best balance of transport
  and telecom reward in the shown sweep.
- Increasing TBS density does not monotonically improve handover-aware throughput: denser THz
  coverage can raise raw rate while also causing more association changes.
- Increasing the number of autonomous vehicles reduces per-vehicle rate and increases handover
  and collision pressure.

**Caveat.** Most result claims are visual/ordinal. There are no confidence intervals, seed-level
dispersion, exact collision-rate tables, or open implementation details sufficient for
independent reproduction.

### Assumptions and limitations

- The road is two-lane and simplified; there are no Manhattan intersections, turns, buildings,
  traffic signals, detailed vehicle dynamics, or non-controlled road users.
- Communication is downlink V2I RF/THz, not NR sidelink or V-VLC.
- Rates are long-block Shannon rates. There are no arrivals, packets, queues, HARQ, deadlines,
  finite-blocklength errors, or reliability constraints.
- Network “actions” select among three hand-written association heuristics rather than choosing a
  BS/resource directly.
- Handover cost is a cumulative scalar penalty, without delay/resource/service interruption.
- Scalar reward weights hide the Pareto trade-off and offer no safety/URLLC constraint guarantee.

### Concrete transfer to the planned RF/VLC/DUP simulator

**Inference / transfer.**

1. Keep communication and mobility causally coupled in the environment, even if the first
   controller only chooses communication. Turns, following distance, lane position, and occluders
   should evolve link geometry rather than appear as independent channel noise.
2. Avoid using a cumulative handover ratio as the only switching model. Implement interruption
   and retain per-episode handover rate as an evaluation metric.
3. If future work jointly controls driving, use a multi-objective or constrained formulation so a
   throughput reward cannot compensate for collisions. For the current scope, freeze an
   externally specified mobility policy to isolate communication control.
4. The non-monotonic density result is a useful test expectation: adding VLC opportunities should
   not automatically help if it also adds interference, switching, contention, or noisy action
   choices.

## P7b — Envelope multi-objective RL in URLLC-enabled vehicular networks

### Exact citation and access

Zijiang Yan and Hina Tabassum, “Generalized Multi-Objective Reinforcement Learning With Envelope
Updates in URLLC-Enabled Vehicular Networks,” *IEEE Transactions on Vehicular Technology*,
vol. 74, no. 11, pp. 17666–17682, November 2025.

- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/TVT.2025.3580502>
- arXiv record: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2405.11331>
- Full-text PDF: <https://arxiv.org/pdf/2405.11331>
- **Access depth:** complete arXiv v3 (14 June 2025) read; final venue/pages checked against IEEE
  metadata.

### Research question and contribution

**Direct.** This is a substantial extension of P7a. It asks how one policy family can cover
different preferences between autonomous-driving performance and V2I communication performance,
instead of retraining one scalarized DQN for every reward weight.

The proposed multi-objective double DQN with envelope updates learns vector-valued returns over a
set of preference vectors. A convex-coverage/envelope Bellman operator chooses the action and
preference-conditioned return maximizing scalar utility. Hindsight/preference replay and a
homotopy loss help train across objectives.

The paper also labels the system URLLC-enabled and replaces Shannon rate with a finite-blocklength
normal approximation. It remains, however, a rate-level model without queues, deadline-expiry
events, or a constrained reliability controller.

### Vehicular and physical models

**Direct.** The environment is a five-lane, 1500 m highway with target controlled AVs and
surrounding vehicles. Longitudinal motion uses kinematic/IDM-style car following, and lane choice
uses MOBIL-style logic.

The cellular tiers remain 3.5 GHz RBS and 1 THz TBS. RF/THz path loss, Rayleigh RF fading,
molecular THz absorption/noise, directional gain, load quota, and handover-adjusted network
quality extend P7a.

Instead of pure Shannon rate, the paper uses

`R = (W/ln 2) [ln(1+gamma) - sqrt(V/(L B)) Q^-1(epsilon_c)]`,

where `V = 1 - (1+gamma)^(-2)`, `L` is blocklength-related transmission duration, `B` bandwidth,
and `epsilon_c` target decoding error. The paper states `D_t = L B`.

**Caveat.** Numerical values for `epsilon_c` and the common blocklength quantity are not supplied
in the main simulation table, preventing exact reproduction of the “URLLC” rate. Claimed
transmission times of about `5e-8 s` for TBS and `4e-7 s` for RBS are many orders below the
15 Hz environment/control step (about 67 ms), and their relationship to a common payload/block
definition is not demonstrated.

### Multi-objective MDP

**Direct.** The state contains target-vehicle arrays/attributes including longitudinal and lateral
position, speed, heading, and counts of available RBS/TBS choices. The combined action is one of
five driving decisions paired with one of three network-selection policies, for 15 actions.

The reward is a two-component vector:

- **transport component:** normalized velocity, collision penalty, right-lane preference, and
  on-road behavior;
- **telecom component:** handover/load-adjusted weighted data rate, scaled by
  `(1 - handover frequency)`.

A preference vector scalarizes the two components at decision/evaluation time.

The envelope algorithm:

1. approximates a vector Q function conditioned on state, action, and preference;
2. takes a generalized Bellman maximum over both next actions and sampled preference vectors;
3. stores/relabels experience for multiple preferences;
4. transitions through a homotopy from vector absolute-error learning to a
   preference-projected utility loss; and
5. uses double-DQN target selection/evaluation plus a target network.

**Caveat.** Deployment is described through a pool of trained models/preferences from which a
suitable policy is selected. This supports runtime preference choice but is not evidence that one
network generalizes robustly to arbitrary unseen dynamics or requirement changes.

### Experimental settings

**Direct.**

| Item | Setting |
|---|---|
| Highway/lane count | 1500 m / 5 |
| Default vehicles | 5 target + 20 surrounding |
| RBS/TBS count | 5 / 10–50 |
| Speed range | 10–35 m/s |
| Maximum episode length | 30 s |
| RBS/TBS carrier | 3.5 GHz / 1 THz |
| RBS/TBS bandwidth | 40 MHz / 500 MHz |
| RBS/TBS user quota | 5 / 10 |
| Transmit power | 1 W |
| THz directional gain/absorption | 316.2 / 0.05 |
| Path-loss exponent | 4 |
| Learning rate/discount | `3e-4` / 0.995 |
| Replay capacity | `2e6` |
| Sampled preference count `N_omega` | 4 |
| Target-network update | every 200 steps |
| Training/evaluation | roughly 4000–4500 / 500 episodes |

Baselines are multi-objective Q-learning, DQN, DDQN, dueling DDQN, PPO, and EUPG-style
policy-gradient learning.

**Caveat.** Architecture specifications conflict internally. A table indicates four hidden layers
of 256 units, while nearby prose describes three layers of 128 units. Exploration, loss, and
preference settings are not all sufficiently detailed to resolve the discrepancy.

### Quantitative findings

**Direct.**

- Relative to weighted multi-objective DQN, the envelope approach is reported to improve average
  transport reward by 12.7%, telecom reward by 18.9%, and a handover-related metric by 12.3%.
- In one learning sequence, collision frequency falls from roughly 0.7 to 0.2.
- A representative table entry with target/surrounding vehicle configuration labelled 15/10 gives
  envelope transport/telecom/total rewards of 25.76/13.45/39.21 versus
  23.42/10.21/33.63 for DQN.
- Reported per-step training/evaluation costs are approximately 0.075/0.065 s for the envelope
  method. Some total timing comparisons are close—for example roughly 30:15 versus 31:10 for
  DQN—so the primary claimed advantage is the learned Pareto coverage, not uniformly lower
  compute.

**Caveat.** “Handover rate improvement” is directionally ambiguous without its exact sign
convention; it should be quoted as the authors’ reported improvement, not automatically as 12.3%
fewer physical handovers. Uncertainty intervals and rare-event reliability counts are absent.

### URLLC assessment

**Direct.** The finite-blocklength penalty explicitly represents a target decoding error in rate
calculation.

**Caveat / assessment.** The evaluation does not specify numerical `epsilon_c`, generate packet
arrivals, maintain queues, expire packet deadlines, model retransmission, or report decoded packet
success-by-deadline. Reward has no reliability/latency constraint or Lagrange multiplier. Thus the
paper offers a finite-blocklength-aware rate model inside a multi-objective driving/network
problem, not an empirical or analytical URLLC guarantee.

### Assumptions and limitations

- Communication is V2I RF/THz, not sidelink RF/VLC.
- The highway lacks urban intersections, buildings, turns, traffic lights, and V-VLC visibility
  geometry.
- No queues, packet traffic, scheduling/PRBs, HARQ, deadline misses, or duplicated copies exist.
- Handover remains a reward modifier rather than an interruption/resource process.
- The scalar preference can trade away either objective; collision and communication reliability
  are not hard constrained.
- Critical finite-blocklength and neural-network parameters are missing or contradictory.
- Performance is primarily average reward without statistical confidence or out-of-distribution
  tests.

### Concrete transfer to the planned RF/VLC/DUP simulator

**Inference / transfer.**

1. Keep vector logging even if the main algorithm is a Lagrangian CMDP: goodput/resource utility,
   deadline-miss cost, RF channel use, VLC use, DUP rate, energy, and switching should remain
   separately observable.
2. Envelope RL is a useful later tool for interactively exploring efficiency preferences, but
   safety/deadline requirements should be constraints. A Pareto point with better average reward
   can still have unacceptable tail reliability.
3. If finite blocklength is included, specify payload bits, channel uses, bandwidth/SCS, target
   decoding error, and mapping from SINR/MCS to BLER. Validate every unit and time scale.
4. Use the paper’s RF/THz complementarity only conceptually. For RF/VLC, model common and
   independent failure causes so DUP diversity is neither unrealistically perfect nor pointless.

## P8 — PPO-based power control in a 5G-connected vehicular network

### Exact citation and access

Mostafa Raeisi and Abu B. Sesay, “Power Control of 5G-Connected Vehicular Network Using
PPO-Based Deep Reinforcement Learning Algorithm,” *IEEE Access*, vol. 12,
pp. 96387–96403, 2024.

- DOI and IEEE record: <https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2024.3427124>
- Public author/full-text page:
  <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/382226920_Power_Control_of_5G-Connected_Vehicular_Network_Using_PPO-based_Deep_Reinforcement_Learning_Algorithm>
- **Access depth:** the complete public accepted manuscript and final article text were read and
  compared. Bibliographic metadata come from the final IEEE record.

### Research question and contribution

**Direct.** The work adjusts each vehicle’s uplink PUSCH EIRP in a 28 GHz 5G V2I system so that
achieved SINR/data rate stays close to a desired target while limiting excess transmit power and
intercell interference. Each roadside gNodeB acts as an agent for the users it serves.

The proposed “modified PPO” removes the learned critic and substitutes a fixed value estimate.
The justification is that every power-control interaction is treated as an independent one-step
episode: the reward depends on the current context/action, not a delayed trajectory return.
Consequently, the implemented problem is closer to a contextual bandit optimized with a clipped
policy-gradient update than a conventional sequential PPO MDP.

This paper is useful as a precedent for standards-available/quantized radio observations and
discrete transmit-power actions. It does not study hybrid RF/VLC, sidelink, packets, queues, or
URLLC deadline reliability.

### Vehicular radio model

**Direct.**

- Uplink V2I uses PUSCH to roadside 5G gNodeBs/small cells.
- The carrier is 28 GHz in band n257/FR2-1.
- Vehicles travel on a straight, two-way road with one lane per direction.
- The nearest LOS roadside unit serves a vehicle.
- The default layout uses eight RSUs. Cell/coverage length is swept from 50 to 500 m.
- Average vehicle speed is 100 km/h with 10 m longitudinal spacing.
- The nominal channel is 100 MHz plus a 2.45 MHz guard allocation.
- Frequencies are reused with a one-RSU separation, and the two strongest cochannel cells are
  represented as dominant interferers.

Large-scale propagation is free-space path loss. Small-scale fading is Rician with reported
parameters `K=9`, scale 0.11, and location 0. Desired received power is divided by the two dominant
interference powers plus thermal noise:

`SINR_i = P_rx,i / (I_1 + I_2 + N)`.

Capacity is

`R_i = B_i log2(1 + SINR_i)`.

**Caveat.** This is a highly favorable mmWave road model: nearest-RSU LOS, free-space loss, and no
buildings, vehicles as blockers, turning, beam acquisition/misalignment, rain, shadowing, HARQ, or
handover interruption.

### Optimization, state, action, and reward

**Direct.** The mathematical objective minimizes

`sum_i |R_i^achieved - R_i^objective|`

subject to minimum/maximum UE EIRP and a minimum SINR requirement. The authors argue that reaching
the target rather than exceeding it minimizes unnecessary power/interference.

**Caveat.** Transmit power is not itself an explicit term in that objective. Target tracking may
often lower power, but minimizing absolute rate error is not mathematically identical to
minimizing energy or aggregate interference.

The per-user observation has 11 normalized/quantized quantities:

1. serving-cell identity;
2. allocated channel;
3. own current EIRP;
4. current PUSCH SINR;
5. own downlink reference-signal path loss;
6. first dominant interferer EIRP;
7. first dominant interferer path loss;
8. second dominant interferer EIRP;
9. second dominant interferer path loss;
10. desired SINR; and
11. RSU coverage/cell length.

Each user action is one of 46 integer EIRP levels from -15 through 30 dBm in 1 dB steps.

The reward is a Gaussian/bell target-tracking curve:

`r = (r_max-r_min) exp[-||X-X_opt||^2/(2g)] + r_min`,

with `r_max=2`, `r_min=-3`, and `g=10`. In the experiments `X` is effectively achieved SINR and
`X_opt` the desired value, so reward peaks at the target and falls on either side.

### Modified PPO and deployment concept

**Direct.** The actor has 11 inputs, seven fully connected hidden layers of 128 tanh units, and 46
linear outputs for the discrete power choices. Inputs are mapped to `[0,1]`. Reported settings are:

- Adam learning rate `3e-5`;
- PPO clip ratio 0.2;
- three training passes per minibatch; and
- KL-divergence stopping threshold 0.01.

At a gNodeB, a current policy performs online inference while a parallel learning process updates
the network from recently collected batches. With one-step episodes, the fixed critic/value term
serves only as a baseline in the clipped update.

**Assessment.** A contextual controller can be appropriate when each channel realization and
target is independent. It cannot learn the delayed effects of transmit power on future
interference, queueing, retransmission, battery, handover, or observations. Those effects make the
planned simulator genuinely sequential and require a state/value model.

### Experimental design and baselines

**Direct.**

| Item | Setting |
|---|---|
| Carrier/band | 28 GHz / n257 FR2-1 |
| Channel allocation | 100 MHz plus 2.45 MHz guard |
| EIRP action set | -15 to 30 dBm in 1 dB steps |
| Main/side antenna gains | 35 / 25 dBi |
| Receiver noise figure/temperature | 10 dB / 300 K |
| Minimum simulation step | 1 ms |
| Road | two lanes total, 3.5 m lane width |
| RSU/vehicle antenna height | 12 / 5 m |
| RSUs/users in principal test | 8 / 150 |
| Default cell length | 100 m |
| Vehicle speed/spacing | 100 km/h / 10 m |

There are four principal baselines:

1. a simplified 3GPP-like closed-loop power controller;
2. maximum power;
3. equal-power policy 1, selected to match a PPO-related mean power; and
4. equal-power policy 2, a fixed-power alternative.

Reported 3GPP settings include 30 dBm maximum, `P0=-60`, UE offset zero, 15 kHz subcarrier
spacing, 50 PRBs, fractional compensation `alpha=0.4`, one layer, and a TPC update weighted by
SINR error with `beta=0.5`.

**Caveat.** The fixed-power descriptions differ between manuscript stages/figures: one version
describes 5 dBm, while plotted discussion elsewhere uses values around -25/0 dB after an
antenna-gain/representation shift. Likewise, some reported average “allocated power” values around
-36 to -34 dB are outside the stated -15–30 dBm EIRP action set. The paper appears to mix EIRP,
gain-adjusted, and/or received-power representations. These values are not safe implementation
defaults without code or author clarification.

### Quantitative findings

**Direct.**

- The principal learning experiment starts 10,000 random configurations with 150 users, eight
  100 m RSUs, and randomized positions, channels, and powers.
- The target represented on the paper’s normalized/plot scale is 90, corresponding to about
  21.5 dB SINR and approximately 715 Mbps.
- After three control steps, both PPO and the 3GPP-like controller have mean output near the target;
  PPO’s reported spread is about 7.1 plotted units versus about 6 for 3GPP.
- After five steps, PPO is reported at mean 90.7 and standard deviation 3.0; after ten steps, mean
  90.4 and standard deviation 2.7. The authors describe practical convergence/stability at around
  six steps.
- Across traffic/user-density and 50–500 m cell-length sweeps, PPO remains closest to the
  715 Mbps target with the lowest or near-lowest variance. The simplified 3GPP controller degrades
  more as load/cell length grows.

**Caveat.** The study provides neither confidence intervals nor independent training seeds.
Training sample count, held-out split, model-selection protocol, hardware, and inference/training
latency are not reported in sufficient detail. Achieving a mean target rate is not evidence about
packet latency or reliability tails.

### Assumptions and limitations

- No RF/VLC hybrid, packet duplication, V2V, PC5 sidelink, urban intersections, or turning is
  included.
- There is no traffic generation, queue, packet size, finite blocklength, decoding error, HARQ,
  deadline, latency violation, or reliability metric.
- Despite broad introductory QoS language, only desired SINR/rate drives the reward.
- Treating samples as one-step episodes removes temporal credit assignment and may make “PPO”
  comparisons with sequential algorithms misleading.
- The closed-loop 3GPP controller is simplified and may be disadvantaged relative to a
  standards-complete implementation.
- Power units/representations are internally inconsistent.
- The radio model omits important 28 GHz blockage and beam-management effects.

### Concrete transfer to the planned RF/VLC/DUP simulator

**Inference / transfer.**

1. Use the 46-level discrete power controller as a reference for action discretization, not as the
   initial main action. Start with `{RF,VLC,DUP}` and fixed well-documented PHY policies; only add
   joint power/MCS control after the link-selection CMDP is validated.
2. Standards-observable features are a strong design principle: sidelink SINR/RSRP, allocated
   resource/channel, transmit-power level, path-loss estimate, interferer/load measures, and
   feature age. Do not expose simulator-only future channel truth.
3. The bell reward is a useful target-tracking baseline, but a deadline-aware controller should
   not punish rate above target symmetrically if extra service reduces an urgent queue. Charge
   resource/power explicitly instead.
4. Preserve time in the MDP. Today’s `{RF,VLC,DUP}` choice affects queues, interference/resource
   occupancy, copy cancellation, and next-step urgency, so a critic/return model is justified.
5. Implement and document a genuinely comparable 3GPP/NR heuristic baseline rather than copying
   this paper’s simplified controller or its inconsistent power numbers.

## Cross-paper synthesis for the planned implementation

### What each paper actually covers

| Entry | Mobility/geometry | Traffic and queue | Reliability/latency treatment | Learned/control action | Most reusable element |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| P1 | static indoor ceiling VLC | none | none | tabular AP association | load-aware association; VLC LOS equation |
| P2 | context/coverage set only | traffic class, no queue | utility thresholds only | tabular LTE/WLAN/VLC choice | action masking and context transfer |
| P3 | indoor random waypoint | none | independent blockage only | sequential PPO AP association | factorized multi-user decisions |
| P4 | static indoor factory | Poisson packets; waiting named | soft latency/outage penalties | AP, subchannel, power via PDS-ERT | latency decomposition and heterogeneous resource accounting |
| P5 | static receiver/abstract switching | ON/OFF Markov; infinite queue | effective-capacity and SNC tails | analytical selection/power split | queue-tail analysis and handover service loss |
| P6 | moving vehicle; abstract overlap CTMC | Poisson finite FIFO | loss/delay/queue cost | CTMDP RF/current-VLC/next-VLC | finite-buffer oracle and predictive handover context |
| P7a | two-lane highway | none | none | joint driving/network heuristic | causal coupling of motion and connectivity |
| P7b | five-lane highway | none | finite-blocklength rate only | multi-objective driving/network action | vector returns/preference analysis |
| P8 | straight two-way 28 GHz road | none | desired SINR/rate only | discrete PUSCH power | standards-observable state/action design |

No paper in P1–P8 implements the target combination of Manhattan urban mobility, physical
NR-sidelink RF, physical V-VLC, per-packet queues/deadlines, the `{RF,VLC,DUP}` action, and a
long-run CMDP reliability constraint. That gap is the rationale for the planned system, but it
also means components must be validated independently rather than assembled without unit tests.

### Minimum defensible environment semantics

**Inference / transfer.** A first implementation should make the following explicit:

1. **Packet lifecycle:** generation timestamp, size/service class, finite queue admission,
   scheduling time, transmission attempt/copy identity, decoding result, first successful arrival,
   cancellation of redundant copy if supported, deadline expiry, and precise drop reason.
2. **RF service:** NR-sidelink time/frequency resource allocation, transmit power/MCS or an
   explicitly scoped abstraction, SINR/interference, BLER mapping, processing/access time, and
   resource use charged per attempt.
3. **VLC service:** transmitter radiation and receiver FoV/orientation, relative pose, range,
   ambient/weather attenuation, geometrical vehicle/building occlusion, optical interference,
   SNR/BLER mapping, and resource use.
4. **DUP semantics:** schedule the same packet on both links; success is
   `RF_success_by_deadline OR VLC_success_by_deadline`; arrival time is the earliest successful
   copy; both scheduled legs consume resources unless cancellation happens soon enough.
5. **Correlated failure:** separate independent per-link noise/interference from common causes
   such as a turn, missing receiver pose, congestion, stale controller state, or processing delay.
6. **Switching:** mode changes cause a physical interruption/control cost and enter the next state.
   Merely subtracting a reward constant is inadequate.
7. **Observation causality:** expose measurable current/past features with an age, never future
   channel outcomes or exact hidden blockage duration.

### Suggested CMDP abstraction

**Inference / transfer.** A compact starting formulation is:

- state `s_t`: relative pose/road phase, RF quality and load, VLC quality/LOS/FoV, RF/VLC
  availability masks, per-link recent outcomes, queue occupancy, head-of-line age/deadline slack,
  service class, previous mode, and feature ages;
- action `a_t in {RF, VLC, DUP}`;
- reward: timely delivered useful bits or packet utility minus normalized RF/VLC resource/energy
  use and switching cost;
- cost: a clearly defined deadline-miss indicator, preferably per generated safety packet;
- constraint: long-run expected miss rate at or below a declared target `epsilon`;
- algorithm: PPO actor/critic with a nonnegative learned Lagrange multiplier, alongside fixed
  penalty and unconstrained PPO ablations.

If a rare target cannot be estimated with the available number of independent packet events,
report that fact. A run with zero observed misses is not evidence of six-nines reliability; give a
binomial confidence bound and the effective number of independent trials.

### Baseline set supported by this batch

**Inference / transfer.**

1. RF-only and VLC-only (P1/P5);
2. strongest instantaneous quality or maximum-rate selection (P1/P3/P7);
3. queue-aware RF/VLC threshold/effective-capacity heuristic (P5);
4. immediate and dwell/hysteresis handover policies (P6);
5. always-DUP and never-DUP;
6. target-tracking/fixed-penalty PPO (P4/P8); and
7. the main Lagrangian/CMDP policy.

Every baseline must receive the same causal observation, PHY/resource budget, packet arrivals,
copy-cancellation rule, and evaluation seeds. Always-DUP is only meaningful if both copies consume
their actual resources and interference externalities.

### Parameters that should not be copied blindly

- P1/P3/P4/P5 optical parameters describe ceiling-lamp indoor LiFi, not headlamp/taillamp V-VLC.
- P4’s factory RF path loss is not an NR-sidelink urban-canyon model.
- P6’s exponential ON/OFF/service rates are useful for an oracle test but not final physical
  calibration.
- P7b omits the finite-blocklength error/blocklength numbers needed for reproduction.
- P8’s EIRP/received/normalized power reporting is inconsistent, and its 28 GHz LOS/free-space
  channel is too simple for Manhattan intersections.
- Fixed reward weights in P4/P7/P8 are experimental choices, not universal safety trade-offs.

### Validation order implied by the literature

**Inference / transfer.**

1. Verify deterministic RF, deterministic VLC, and queue timestamp/accounting tests.
2. Verify V-VLC geometry/FoV/occlusion against hand-calculated link cases.
3. Verify RF SINR/BLER/resource accounting against a documented NR reference configuration.
4. Verify duplication OR-success, earliest-arrival, and dual-resource charging.
5. Reproduce a small P5/P6-style queue/CTMDP case with an analytical or value-iteration oracle.
6. Establish heuristic/unconstrained/fixed-penalty baselines.
7. Train the CMDP policy, evaluate on held-out seeds and shifted density/weather/obstruction
   settings, and report means, quantiles, deadline-miss confidence intervals, constraint trajectory,
   and resource/duplication/switching rates.

This order separates simulator correctness from policy performance. It also prevents a plausible
reward curve from being mistaken for a reliable communication system.
