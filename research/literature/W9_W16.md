# Deep-reading notes: workbook entries W9-W16

These notes were prepared from the workbook
`RF_VLC_RL_literature_review.xlsx`, then checked against primary publication
pages and, wherever possible, the full paper. They deliberately distinguish:

- **direct evidence**: a result, equation, parameter, or claim in the paper;
- **author interpretation**: a conclusion stated by the paper's authors;
- **our inference**: a coding or research implication that is not itself tested
  by the paper.

This matters because W9-W12 are surveys, W13-W16 solve different technical
problems, and none of W9-W16 implements the complete hybrid RF/VLC V2V CMDP
posed in the workbook.

## W9 — Towards 6G C-V2X Networks: A Comprehensive Survey on Mobility Management, Multi-RAT Coexistence, and Machine Learning (3M) Framework for C-ITS

### Citation and access

- M. A. Ali, S. A. Khan, S. Aldirmaz Colak, S. Kosunalp, and T. Iliev,
  “Towards 6G C-V2X Networks: A Comprehensive Survey on Mobility Management,
  Multi-RAT Coexistence, and Machine Learning (3M) Framework for C-ITS,”
  *Electronics*, vol. 15, no. 5, art. 1042, 2026.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.3390/electronics15051042>
- Publisher full text:
  <https://www.mdpi.com/2079-9292/15/5/1042>
- Access depth: publisher full text was indexed and the major argument,
  taxonomy, tables, and framework sections were inspected. This is a survey
  and conceptual architecture, not an experimental paper.

### Research purpose

The paper tries to unify three bodies of work that are often reviewed
separately:

1. mobility and handover management;
2. coexistence and selection among multiple radio access technologies; and
3. machine-learning-assisted control.

The proposed “3M” framework is a feedback architecture. Vehicle position,
velocity, received signal strength, and other context feed learning modules;
those modules produce proactive handover and RAT-selection decisions. The
paper's conceptual progression is from reactive threshold rules to predictive,
context-aware control.

### Technologies and scope

- Core technologies are DSRC/IEEE 802.11p, LTE-V2X, NR-V2X, the PC5 sidelink,
  the Uu cellular interface, MEC, and multi-connectivity.
- The paper argues that no single RAT can reliably satisfy every CAM/DENM
  requirement across all traffic and topology regimes.
- Its hybrid focus is predominantly **RF multi-RAT coexistence**. A full-text
  search did not reveal a developed RF/VLC control model or a vehicular VLC
  evaluation.

This makes W9 useful as a mobility and multi-RAT survey, but it is not direct
evidence about optical blockage, vehicle orientation, VLC geometry, or
RF/VLC switching.

### Main technical ideas

#### Mobility management

- Dense small cells and high vehicle speed create frequent handovers,
  ping-pong behavior, signaling overhead, and handover failures.
- MEC is presented as a controller that can use vehicle position, heading,
  speed, and acceleration to anticipate arrival at a target cell and reserve a
  handover opportunity before the serving link fails.
- Multi-connectivity is presented as a resilience mechanism: a vehicle can
  maintain more than one attachment, including a PC5 sidelink and Uu
  infrastructure link, instead of relying on a single break-before-make event.

#### Multi-RAT management

- Coexistence is motivated by heterogeneous service requirements and by the
  inability of a single radio to cover every density, mobility, and coverage
  condition.
- The survey emphasizes context-aware RAT choice rather than a fixed
  technology hierarchy.
- Relevant decision features include load, bandwidth, link quality,
  application QoS, mobility, and predicted connectivity.

#### Learning

- Value-based RL is described as suitable for discrete network-selection
  choices.
- Policy-based DRL is described as more appropriate for continuous controls
  such as transmit power.
- LSTM/DL trajectory or link prediction is proposed as an input to proactive
  handover.
- These are architectural recommendations assembled from prior work; the
  paper does not train and test one complete 3M agent end to end.

### What the paper directly establishes

- It documents a fragmented research landscape: multi-RAT, handover, and ML
  are often studied independently.
- It identifies insufficient empirical validation in dense urban conditions
  and weak evidence of generalization across mobility/topology regimes.
- It highlights unresolved interoperability, dynamic spectrum allocation,
  interference, signaling, and security issues.
- It argues for multi-objective decisions rather than optimizing a single QoS
  statistic.

### Claims that should not be carried into code as established facts

- The text uses aspirational 6G targets, including very high reliability, as
  design goals. Those targets are not empirical outcomes of a 3M
  implementation.
- Statements about a particular ML family being best are survey-level
  guidance, not controlled comparisons under a shared V2X simulator.
- “Proactive” is an architectural property here; the paper does not provide a
  calibrated uncertainty model or a reproducible prediction horizon for the
  intended RF/VLC task.

### Limitations

- No new simulator, dataset, policy, or field trial.
- No VLC channel or RF/VLC action space.
- Broad technology coverage produces uneven evidence depth.
- Some forward-looking performance language is stronger than the paper's own
  evaluation base.

### Coding relevance

**Our inference:** W9 supports an architecture with separate estimation,
prediction, and constrained decision layers:

```text
measured context -> belief/forecast -> link or duplication action
                 -> cost/reliability accounting
```

It also supports testing the eventual controller across traffic density,
mobility, and topology distributions rather than only one nominal Manhattan
scenario. It does **not** supply the RF/VLC propagation model or a defensible
URLLC reward by itself.

## W10 — A Survey of Handover Approaches in Visible Light-Based V2X Communications

### Citation and access

- S. Hajbi, A. Kharbouche, M. Zhour, and Y. Zouine, “A Survey of Handover
  Approaches in Visible Light-Based V2X Communications,” in *Progress in
  Intelligent Computing and Secure Communication Systems*, pp. 99-109, 2025.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-031-99997-0_10>
- Publisher page:
  <https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-3-031-99997-0_10>
- Access depth: abstract, bibliographic metadata, and complete reference list.
  The chapter body was paywalled and no lawful public author manuscript was
  found. This is therefore an **abstract/reference-map review**, not a
  full-text deep read.

### What can be established

- The chapter is explicitly about handover decision and execution in
  visible-light V2X.
- Its motivating problems are high speed, short optical coverage,
  line-of-sight interruption, and signal fluctuation.
- Its references map the principal vehicular-VLC handover line:
  vehicle-to-infrastructure VLC handover, relay-assisted handover,
  prediction-assisted VLC/IEEE 802.11p switching, hybrid VLC/RF handover, and
  optical channel/mobility studies.

### What cannot be established from available access

- The chapter's exact taxonomy, comparison criteria, inclusion protocol,
  numerical tables, and detailed conclusions.
- Whether it independently re-derives any model or merely summarizes prior
  work.
- Any numerical claim attributed only to this chapter.

### Coding relevance

W10 is useful as a **bibliographic hub**, especially for W6/W19/W20 and the
underlying V2I-VLC handover literature. It should not be cited as the source of
a physical parameter, reward, or performance improvement unless the chapter
body is supplied later.

## W11 — Handover decision techniques for vehicle-to-vehicle communication in 6G networks: exploring applications, challenges, future trends ahead

### Citation and access

- M. Aram et al., “Handover decision techniques for vehicle-to-vehicle
  communication in 6G networks: exploring applications, challenges, future
  trends ahead,” *EURASIP Journal on Wireless Communications and Networking*,
  art. 11, 2026.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1186/s13638-025-02527-z>
- Full text:
  <https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13638-025-02527-z>
- Local copy: `research/literature/pdfs/W11.pdf`
- Access depth: full paper.

### Research purpose and taxonomy

This is a broad narrative survey of handover decisions for high-mobility V2X
and envisioned 6G systems. It covers:

- conventional threshold methods;
- Bayesian and fuzzy methods;
- machine learning and reinforcement learning;
- software-defined networking;
- multi-connectivity;
- high-frequency links, including mmWave/THz; and
- security, interference, service continuity, and mobility prediction.

Despite “V2V” in the title, much of the described handover machinery is the
usual vehicle-to-infrastructure or vehicle-to-network association problem.
That distinction matters for a direct V2V RF/VLC implementation.

### Useful design concepts

- Useful handover context includes speed, location/trajectory, channel
  observations, neighbor/target availability, and service/QoS demand.
- Predictive decisions can reduce the lag of RSS-threshold handovers in
  fast-changing channels.
- Dual connectivity and make-before-break operation can preserve service while
  a fragile high-frequency link is lost.
- THz/mmWave links make blockage and beam alignment central to mobility
  management, so a controller should distinguish link degradation causes.

### Evidence quality

- The work synthesizes prior literature; it does not propose and evaluate a
  new MDP.
- It contains a generic statement that DNN/RL decisions can occur on the order
  of 5-20 ms. The statement is not tied to a reproducible benchmark in the
  chapter and should **not** be treated as a verified inference-time bound.
- VLC is not developed as a modeled RAT; it appears only peripherally/future
  facing. The paper is therefore not direct evidence for RF/VLC handover.

### Limitations

- V2V, V2I, and V2N settings are not always cleanly separated.
- Performance claims inherited from heterogeneous sources are not normalized
  to common workloads, hardware, channel models, or latency definitions.
- The survey does not supply the equations or parameters needed for
  implementation.

### Coding relevance

**Our inference:** W11 supports including trajectory and outage-cause context,
and evaluating make-before-break or duplication policies. It cannot justify
the physical channel, the exact switching delay, or a policy's latency budget.

## W12 — Multi-Technology Integration in Hybrid V2X Networks: A Survey, Challenges, and Open Issues

### Citation and access

- S. Baccari, M. Hadded, H. Touati, H. Ghazzai, A. Laouiti, and G. Setti,
  “Multi-Technology Integration in Hybrid V2X Networks: A Survey, Challenges,
  and Open Issues,” *IEEE Access*, vol. 13, pp. 138475-138508, 2025.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/ACCESS.2025.3595461>
- Repository record:
  <https://repository.kaust.edu.sa/items/e3e3ed0d-53a1-41a0-b797-5d09ff7d7d5e>
- Indexed full text:
  <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/394303497_Multi-Technology_Integration_in_Hybrid_V2X_Networks_A_Survey_Challenges_and_Open_Issues>
- Access depth: the repository PDF endpoint was blocked to the command-line
  client, but the CC-BY full article was indexed and the taxonomy, relevant
  literature sections, qualitative analysis, open issues, and reference list
  were inspected.

### Definition and organization

The paper defines a hybrid/heterogeneous V2X network as one using at least two
communication technologies to exploit their complementary strengths. Its
review is organized along three solution axes:

1. resource management;
2. handover management; and
3. security and privacy.

It also separates deterministic/rule-based methods from learning-based methods
for resource and handover management.

### Search protocol

The authors report searches of Google Scholar plus IEEE Xplore,
ScienceDirect, MDPI, Springer, Elsevier, and Wiley. Search terms include
heterogeneous/hybrid/multi-technology V2X, combinations of DSRC/ITS-G5 and
C-V2X, RF-VLC, resource management, resource allocation, vertical handover,
and security/privacy. Their main inclusion period starts in 2020 and excludes
single-technology studies.

This makes the paper valuable for recent systems-level hybrid-V2X coverage,
but it intentionally omits some older foundational RF/VLC papers.

### Main integration challenges

- PHY/MAC and message-format heterogeneity;
- resource allocation across interfaces;
- seamless vertical handover and avoidance of ping-pong/unnecessary
  switching;
- cross-technology security, authentication, certificate, and privacy
  compatibility;
- interoperability and translation between standards;
- regulatory and spectrum fragmentation; and
- scalability with vehicle density and traffic demand.

The paper points out that 802.11p and C-V2X are not natively interoperable;
dual-radio OBUs or edge translation are practical bridges but introduce
latency, complexity, and scalability concerns.

### Quantitative survey findings

These values describe the **survey's classified sample**, not universal
population statistics:

- about 87% of reviewed solutions were simulation based and 13% used
  real-world experiments;
- only about 6% were rated “good” for scalability;
- about 38% were rated “good” for evolutivity;
- 48% of the surveyed solutions addressed resource management, versus 15%
  addressing security;
- about 32% of resource-management and 35% of handover-management solutions
  were learning based;
- two-technology hybrids represented about 61% of reviewed integrations; and
- C-V2X plus 802.11p dominated, while VLC and satellite integration were
  comparatively sparse.

The authors use qualitative low/medium/good ratings for scalability,
adaptability, mobility support, and evolutivity. Those ratings involve expert
judgment and should not be read as measured effect sizes.

### Open issues most relevant to this project

- More testing under high load, interference, extreme weather, and varied
  real-world conditions.
- Large-scale field trials beyond small controlled testbeds.
- Adaptive, computationally efficient AI that can run within a real-time
  vehicle budget.
- Predictive handover using history, mobility, bandwidth, and link-reliability
  context.
- Models that scale to dense traffic and remain interoperable with emerging
  6G technologies.
- Joint treatment of resource, handover, and security costs rather than
  isolated optimization.

### What W12 does and does not establish

It strongly supports the **research gap**: hybrid V2X work is simulation-heavy,
mostly two-RAT, and underexplores learning-based VLC integration. It does not
provide a new RF/VLC MDP, physical layer, simulator, or controlled result.

### Coding relevance

**Our inference:** use W12 to define evaluation axes and stress tests:
scalability, environmental adaptability, mobility, integration extensibility,
handover continuity, and computational budget. Do not use the survey's
qualitative ratings as reward weights.

## W13 — Machine Learning-Based Handovers for Sub-6 GHz and mmWave Integrated Vehicular Networks

### Citation and access

- L. Yan, H. Ding, L. Zhang, J. Liu, X. Fang, Y. Fang, M. Xiao, and X. Huang,
  “Machine Learning-Based Handovers for Sub-6 GHz and mmWave Integrated
  Vehicular Networks,” *IEEE Transactions on Wireless Communications*, vol.
  18, no. 10, pp. 4873-4885, 2019.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/TWC.2019.2930193>
- Local full text: `research/literature/pdfs/W13.pdf`
- Access depth: full paper.

### System architecture

The paper uses control/user-plane decoupling:

- a reliable sub-6 GHz macro layer carries control and supplies low-frequency
  channel-state information;
- mmWave roadside radio units provide high-rate user-plane service;
- centralized/cloud coordination and X3-like links allow pre-activation and
  handover preparation.

The central insight is that sub-6 GHz channel fingerprints and current mmWave
beam context contain information about vehicle location and the next mmWave
attachment.

### Three proposed mechanisms

#### 1. Position learning

A kernel ridge regression with a Gaussian kernel maps sub-6 GHz CSI
fingerprints to vehicle position. Predicted position is used to activate a
small relevant subset of mmWave RRUs rather than every possible target.

#### 2. Target RRU and beam prediction

K-nearest-neighbor classification takes:

- estimated position;
- source mmWave-RRU identity; and
- serving beam identities at both RRU and vehicle

and predicts the target RRU/beam pair. The objective is to bypass exhaustive
beam training during handover.

#### 3. V2V bi-casting during hard handover

A co-directional nearby vehicle receives a duplicate stream and relays it
during interruption, reducing the service gap of the mmWave hard handover.

### Analytic model

The paper also models vehicle arrivals/locations on lanes using Poisson
processes and analyzes the probability and duration of a suitable V2V
link-holding interval.

### Important simulation parameters

- sub-6 GHz: 2 GHz, 20 MHz, 43 dBm, approximately 500 m coverage;
- mmWave: 30 GHz, 1 GHz bandwidth, 30 dBm;
- 64 beam choices;
- modeled handover time: 0.5 s;
- example lane speeds: 30 m/s and 20 m/s;
- KNN training example: 50 samples.

### Results

Directly reported results include:

- mean/representative localization error on the order of 0.62 m in one
  coordinate and 0.17 m in the other for the illustrated setting;
- near-zero KNN target-prediction error after a physically meaningful beam
  renumbering with a small neighborhood (reported example \(K=4\));
- decision time around 1.95-2.40 ms, compared with 12.8-40.96 ms for the
  beam-training alternatives considered; and
- reduced interruption with V2V bi-casting.

These figures belong to the paper's structured highway and codebook setting;
they are not Manhattan generalization results.

### Limitations

- Supervised prediction, not reinforcement learning.
- The action is mmWave handover/beam preparation, not RF/VLC selection.
- Requires a fingerprint database and consistent infrastructure/beam
  identities.
- Relatively clean highway geometry and limited domain-shift evaluation.
- No CMDP, tail-latency constraint, or calibrated uncertainty for
  out-of-distribution trajectories.

### Coding relevance

**Our inference:** W13 is strong evidence that prediction should use geometry
and context available on the vehicle, and that a low-frequency/reliable link
can assist a fragile high-rate link. For Manhattan code, target identifiers and
beam/orientation features must be designed so that symmetries are learnable;
the paper's favorable KNN result after beam renumbering is a warning that
representation design can dominate algorithm choice.

## W14 — Intelligent Handover Algorithm for Vehicle-to-Network Communications With Double-Deep Q-Learning

### Citation and access

- Y. Tan et al., “Intelligent Handover Algorithm for Vehicle-to-Network
  Communications With Double-Deep Q-Learning,” *IEEE Transactions on
  Vehicular Technology*, vol. 71, no. 7, pp. 7848-7862, 2022.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/TVT.2022.3169804>
- Local full text: `research/literature/pdfs/W14.pdf`
- Access depth: full paper.

### Problem

The study improves LTE hard-handover timing and target selection for a vehicle
moving through an urban macro network. It treats each handover decision as a
discrete DDQN action.

### MDP

- **State:** received reference-signal powers from all modeled base stations
  plus a one-hot representation of the current serving base station.
- **Action:** select one base station; selecting the current base station means
  stay.
- **Reward:** favors high serving signal quality and penalizes unnecessary
  switching.
- **Execution:** a network is trained offline and then used online when the
  standard A3 measurement event triggers decision logic.

The state uses standard radio measurements, which is practical, but assumes
the vehicle can observe the complete candidate-BS vector.

### Experimental setup

- ns-3 LTE/EPC Network Simulator, LENA components;
- Glasgow map, approximately 2 km by 2 km;
- 8 base stations;
- 18 routes derived from Google route information;
- nominal vehicle speed 60 km/h;
- DDQN with three hidden layers and GELU activation;
- AdamW optimizer, learning rate 0.001;
- replay memory 100,000;
- batch size 1024;
- discount/decay value reported as 0.98.

### Results

- In the paper's illustrative route, DDQN triggers a handover about 10.04 s
  earlier than the conventional decision and attains more than 12.5 dB maximum
  SINR improvement.
- Packet loss in that example falls from 30 to 13 packets.
- Across 47 handovers, the reported cumulative reduction is 1,741 lost
  packets, or 42.62%; the average per-handover packet-loss reduction is
  reported as 25.72%.
- Mean trigger-time improvement is about 11.56 s.
- The learned method incurs one additional handover in the aggregate
  comparison.

### Strengths

- Uses a real urban map and multiple routes rather than a single analytic
  straight line.
- Evaluates packet loss, not just reward.
- Provides a reproducible standard-measurement state.
- DDQN directly addresses DQN overestimation in discrete selection.

### Limitations

- LTE V2N, not direct V2V.
- No VLC, optical blockage, or hybrid-radio duplication.
- “All BS RSRPs” may not be observable at the intended rate in a decentralized
  direct V2V controller.
- Training/deployment is region specific; transfer to unseen layouts is not
  demonstrated.
- Switch penalty is a soft reward term, not a constrained reliability
  guarantee.
- Mean/cumulative packet counts do not establish extreme-tail URLLC
  reliability.

### Coding relevance

**Our inference:** W14 is a strong urban mobility and baseline-design precedent.
Use standard observable features, include a handover cost, compare against an
A3-like hysteresis/time-to-trigger rule, and report both service outcomes and
handover count. Its state should not be copied wholesale if our agent lacks a
global RF measurement vector.

## W15 — 6G-AUTOR: Autonomic CSI-Free Transceiver via Realtime On-Device Signal Analytics

### Citation and access

- C. Lin et al., “6G-AUTOR: Autonomic CSI-Free Transceiver via Realtime
  On-Device Signal Analytics,” arXiv:2206.03250, 2022.
- Primary preprint: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2206.03250>
- Local full text: `research/literature/pdfs/W15.pdf`
- Access depth: full paper.

### Paper scope

6G-AUTOR is a broader on-device transceiver framework. The workbook-relevant
component is DIRM, a DRL-based interference/resource-management mechanism for
several transmitter-receiver pairs.

It is **not** a handover or hybrid link-selection paper.

### DIRM MDP

Each transmitter acts independently with a DQN:

- **local state/observation:** whether the transmitter sent, previous received
  signal power, previous interference, exploration/episode context, with an
  observation history/noise treatment described in the paper;
- **action:** choose a resource block and one discrete transmit-power level,
  including an effectively-off value;
- **reward:** shared sum spectral efficiency.

The discrete power set in the experiment is
\([-100, 5, 15, 23]\) dBm. With \(M\) resource blocks and an off/selection
encoding, the action space is built from resource and power combinations.
Agents use independent Double-DQNs, replay, target networks, and epsilon-greedy
exploration.

“CSI-free” means that current explicit CSI is not provided to the decision
agent; it does **not** mean the agent is measurement-free. Previous received
power and interference are central observations.

### Simulation

- Manhattan area: 300 m by 200 m;
- 6 vehicles / three communicating pairs;
- speed: 45 mph;
- 3 resource blocks, each 180 kHz;
- 5.9 GHz NR Mode 2 abstraction;
- SUMO mobility update: 0.1 s;
- communication scheduling interval: 1 ms;
- DQN hidden layers: 400, 200, 150, tanh;
- RMSProp learning rate \(10^{-4}\);
- replay memory 25,000;
- batch size 200;
- discount 0.995;
- target update every 5 steps;
- epsilon 1 to 0.2;
- 5,000 training episodes.

### Results

In the reported test:

- brute-force benchmark: about 685 kbit/s per pair, zero collision/failure;
- DIRM: about 665.5 kbit/s per pair, 0.7% collision and 0.4% failure;
- random selection: about 280 kbit/s per pair, 31.9% collision and 16.6%
  failure.

### Limitations

- Very small topology and simplified channel/resource setting.
- Sum spectral efficiency is not a URLLC/CMDP objective.
- No RF/VLC, no link switching, no optical blockage.
- Failure rates around \(10^{-3}\) to \(10^{-2}\) in a small experiment do not
  establish rare-event safety.
- A 1 ms scheduler does not imply 1 ms end-to-end latency.
- Brute force is tractable only because the scenario is tiny.

### Coding relevance

**Our inference:** W15 is useful for a SUMO/Manhattan timing example,
decentralized previous-measurement features, and a brute-force small-case
oracle. It should be classified as **resource/power allocation**, not as
evidence that DDQN solves hybrid RF/VLC link selection.

## W16 — Deep Reinforcement Learning and Permissioned Blockchain for Content Caching in Vehicular Edge Computing and Networks

### Citation and access

- Y. Dai et al., “Deep Reinforcement Learning and Permissioned Blockchain for
  Content Caching in Vehicular Edge Computing and Networks,”
  arXiv:2011.08449.
- Primary preprint: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2011.08449>
- Local full text: `research/literature/pdfs/W16.pdf`
- Access depth: full paper.

### Problem

Vehicles with cached content act as providers for requesting vehicles. The
controller assigns provider-requester pairs while accounting for data rate,
deadline, energy, direction, content availability, and storage.

The blockchain component supplies a permissioned trust/accounting layer; the
DRL component performs combinatorial assignment.

### Mobility and channel abstraction

The paper first writes a stylized Manhattan-grid mobility model with direction
and movement probabilities. Its numerical evaluation then uses an Uber
Manhattan vehicle-location dataset rather than a microscopic SUMO traffic
simulation.

This distinction corrects the workbook implication that W16 is a SUMO
Manhattan controller.

### DRL construction

- **State:** a global vector containing pairwise communication rates/latencies,
  energy terms, vehicle direction, requests/content availability, deadlines,
  and cache capacities.
- **Action:** provider-requester association.
- **Algorithm:** DDPG emits a continuous proto-action; a Hungarian assignment
  maps it to a feasible discrete matching.
- **Reward/utility:** successful assignments obtain the stated utility;
  constraint-violating solutions receive a fixed negative penalty.

This is penalty-based constrained behavior, not a formal CMDP with separately
tracked cost returns and a learned dual variable.

### Evaluation parameters

- approximately 1.52 km² Manhattan area from Uber traces;
- 100 vehicles, including 50 candidate providers;
- content size 10-50 MB;
- deadlines 5-10 s;
- communication range 500 m;
- transmit power 24 dBm;
- bandwidth 10 MHz;
- 4,000 episodes with 20 steps per episode;
- fixed infeasibility penalty -100;
- reported learning rate \(10^{-2}\).

### Results

The learned matching attains a service/success rate above roughly 86% in the
reported setting, versus about 78% for the greedy benchmark and approximately
5% for random matching. Exact values vary with the plotted scenario.

### Limitations

- Content delivery with multi-second deadlines, not URLLC safety messaging.
- Global state and centralized association.
- Manhattan data points do not model interactions, car following, lane
  changes, traffic signals, or blockage in the way SUMO would.
- No VLC and no hybrid link choice.
- A fixed negative reward for violation neither guarantees feasibility nor
  calibrates the probability of rare violations.
- Blockchain latency/overhead and radio-control latency are different parts of
  the system and should not be conflated.

### Coding relevance

**Our inference:** W16 demonstrates a useful pattern for mixed
continuous/discrete combinatorial actions: produce scores or a proto-action,
then project through a feasibility solver. It can inspire a safety layer or
action mask, but it is not a direct link-selection baseline.

## Cross-entry conclusions from W9-W16

### Directly reusable ideas

- W13: cross-band/context prediction, compact candidate activation, and
  make-before-break/bi-casting.
- W14: observable radio-measurement state, explicit switching penalty, DDQN,
  urban route evaluation, and conventional handover baselines.
- W15: asynchronous local measurements, resource/power action factorization,
  small-case brute-force oracle, and SUMO communication/mobility timescale
  separation.
- W16: projection of a learned proposal onto a discrete feasible assignment.
- W9/W11/W12: evaluation axes, prediction motivation, multi-connectivity, and
  the need for generalization and real-world stress tests.

### Non-transferable assumptions to avoid

- Treating survey aspirations as measured results.
- Treating V2N handover as identical to direct V2V switching.
- Calling prior received signal/interference “no channel information.”
- Treating a scheduling interval as end-to-end latency.
- Treating a reward penalty as a CMDP guarantee.
- Treating a Manhattan coordinate dataset as microscopic traffic simulation.
- Treating mean packet loss or a modest empirical failure rate as evidence for
  a \(10^{-5}\)-level reliability constraint.

### Resulting gap

Across W9-W16, no paper jointly supplies:

- direct V2V RF/VLC physics and blockage;
- partial observability plus a calibrated near-future link forecast;
- explicit RF-only, VLC-only, and duplication actions;
- switching and duplication costs;
- a formal long-run/tail reliability constraint;
- SUMO Manhattan mobility;
- and validation under distribution shift with rare-event confidence bounds.

That conjunction, rather than “PPO has never been used,” is the defensible
implementation/research gap.
