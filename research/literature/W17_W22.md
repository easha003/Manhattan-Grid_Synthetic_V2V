# Deep-reading notes: workbook entries W17-W22

These notes use the same evidence convention as `W9_W16.md`: claims are
separated into direct full-text evidence, author interpretation, and our
implementation inference. W17-W22 span a survey, a physical optical-channel
model, two hybrid handover heuristics, a high-frequency outage classifier, and
a cellular-V2X DRL resource controller. They are complementary rather than
interchangeable.

## W17 — Vehicular Visible Light Communications: A Survey

### Citation and access

- A. Memedi and F. Dressler, “Vehicular Visible Light Communications: A
  Survey,” *IEEE Communications Surveys & Tutorials*, vol. 23, no. 1,
  pp. 161-181, 2021.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/COMST.2020.3034224>
- Author page: <https://www.ccs-labs.org/bib/memedi2021vehicular/>
- Local full text: `research/literature/pdfs/W17.pdf`
- Access depth: full paper.

### Purpose

W17 surveys the entire vehicular-VLC stack:

- applications and requirements;
- optical transmitters and receivers;
- propagation/channel modeling;
- modulation and coding;
- MAC/network behavior;
- experiments, simulators, and open problems.

It is the most useful entry in this batch for checking whether the optical
model used in future code resembles an outdoor vehicular link rather than an
indoor ceiling-light model.

### Why VLC is complementary to RF

Directly from the survey's synthesis:

- visible light offers very large unlicensed spectrum;
- cars and road infrastructure already contain LED sources;
- optical directionality and a small collision domain can reduce interference
  and spatially reuse spectrum;
- but VLC needs an adequate optical path and has short, geometry-sensitive
  coverage;
- opaque blockers, orientation, weather, sunlight, and dirty optical surfaces
  can severely degrade it.

The paper therefore treats vehicular VLC primarily as a complement to RF, not
as a universal replacement.

### Applications and latency/reliability interpretation

The paper covers cooperative awareness/perception, emergency braking,
traffic-information services, intersection assistance, and platoons. Its
application summary gives hard-real-time values generally on the order of
less than 100 ms for platooning/emergency cases and reliability requirements
above roughly 95% for some classes.

Those are application-table values from a survey, not evidence of
microsecond-level optical URLLC. They should not be silently converted into a
\(10^{-5}\) or \(10^{-6}\) outage target.

### Optical channel factors

#### Transmitter

- Headlamps and taillamps have asymmetric, manufacturer- and function-specific
  radiation patterns.
- Treating a headlamp as a generic Lambertian ceiling LED can create a
  fundamentally wrong angular gain.
- LED bandwidth and driver circuitry constrain modulation.

#### Receiver

- PIN photodiodes are common and simple; avalanche photodiodes offer gain with
  added bias/noise/complexity.
- A wider field of view improves alignment tolerance but admits more ambient
  light and interference.
- Imaging/camera receivers can spatially separate sources, but commodity frame
  rates (the survey discusses roughly 30-100 frames/s) limit throughput and
  temporal response.

#### Geometry and motion

- Distance, relative yaw/pitch, lane offset, road curvature, vehicle height,
  and vibration change irradiance/incidence angles.
- V2V headlamp-to-rear-receiver and taillamp-to-front-receiver links are
  asymmetric.
- Fast changes can occur without a large change in Euclidean distance.

#### Noise, obstruction, and weather

- Sunlight produces shot noise and can saturate the receiver.
- Artificial lights produce structured interference/flicker.
- Fog, rain, and snow attenuate/scatter light; dirty lamps/receivers can be
  decisive in practice.
- The paper discusses Mie-scattering and visibility-based weather models.
- Ground/reflected paths are not always simply harmful: a reflected component
  may add received energy, and road wetness/weather changes reflectivity.

### Canonical link model

The survey uses the usual optical relation

\[
P_r = H(0)P_t,
\]

with a DC channel gain determined by source radiation, distance, detector
area, incidence/irradiance geometry, optical filtering/concentration, and
field of view. Shot and thermal noise then determine SNR/BER.

For implementation, the important lesson is not one universal coefficient but
the decomposition of physical causes and the domains in which each
approximation is valid.

### Simulators and reproducibility

At the time of the review, Veins-VLC was identified as the principal open
source vehicular-network/VLC simulator, while many results used closed or
one-off tools. The paper argues that proprietary/non-released simulators
impede reproducibility.

### Limitations

- Survey, not a new policy or experiment.
- Many underlying studies use incompatible devices, channel assumptions, and
  metrics.
- Published vehicular-VLC results were dominated by PHY/channel studies, with
  much less evidence for complete network and transport stacks.
- No RL/CMDP.

### Coding relevance

**Our inference:** W17 should become a model-review checklist:

1. realistic non-Lambertian or measured automotive radiation pattern;
2. relative position and orientation;
3. detector area/FOV and receiver type;
4. ambient and receiver noise;
5. explicit opaque-vehicle blockage;
6. weather/visibility regime;
7. asymmetric forward/rear optical links; and
8. reproducible parameters and sensitivity sweeps.

An initial Lambertian model may be acceptable as a declared abstraction, but
it should be stress-tested against non-Lambertian patterns before claiming
vehicular realism.

## W18 — Infrastructure-to-Vehicle Visible Light Communications: Channel Modelling and Performance Analysis

### Citation and access

- H. B. Eldeeb, M. Elamassie, S. M. Sait, and M. Uysal,
  “Infrastructure-to-Vehicle Visible Light Communications: Channel Modelling
  and Performance Analysis,” *IEEE Transactions on Vehicular Technology*,
  vol. 71, no. 3, pp. 2240-2250, 2022.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/TVT.2022.3142991>
- Indexed full text:
  <https://www.researchgate.net/publication/357818828_Infrastructure-to-Vehicle_Visible_Light_Communications_Channel_Modelling_and_Performance_Analysis>
- Access depth: complete indexed manuscript text, equations, parameter ranges,
  and result discussions were inspected.

### Problem and simulation method

The study builds a tractable I2V streetlight-to-car VLC channel model:

- a two-lane highway;
- light poles uniformly placed on both sides;
- a photodetector mounted on top of the vehicle;
- the four nearest light sources included;
- non-sequential OpticStudio ray tracing;
- a commercial Vestel Ephesus M4S streetlight with an asymmetric radiation
  pattern;
- wavelength-dependent material reflectance; and
- clear-weather atmospheric modeling using Mie scattering.

This is a significant improvement over an ideal Lambertian streetlight and a
single LOS ray.

### Closed-form fitted path loss

The ray-traced received power varies periodically as the car passes uniformly
spaced poles. The authors fit path loss in dB using a sinusoidal form:

\[
h_{\mathrm{dB}}(d)
  = C_P\cos\!\left(\frac{2\pi d}{d_T}\right)+C_{SA},
\]

where \(d_T\) is pole spacing. The sinusoid's amplitude and offset are
polynomial functions of:

- receiver aperture \(D_r\);
- pole height \(H_P\);
- vehicle height \(H_V\);
- pole spacing \(d_T\);
- lateral distance/shift \(d_h\); and
- fitted coefficients.

Because vehicle position within a pole-spacing interval is treated as uniform,
the periodic coefficient has an arcsine-type probability density. The authors
use this to derive the SNR distribution and an approximate PAM BER through
Gauss-Chebyshev quadrature.

### Parameter domain

The fit is built over approximately:

- pole heights: 6-10 m;
- pole spacing: 15-30 m;
- detector apertures: 1, 2.5, and 4 cm;
- vehicle heights: 0.8, 1.37, and 1.9 m;
- lateral offsets: 1-2.5 m;
- FOV: 65-90 degrees.

The example BER study uses:

- 4-PAM;
- 5 MHz bandwidth;
- noise spectral density \(10^{-21}\ \mathrm{A^2/Hz}\);
- electro-optical conversion factor 0.8 W/A; and
- detector responsivity 0.4 A/W.

### Results

- Receiver aperture is the dominant examined transceiver factor.
- For one mid-cell comparison, path loss is about -59.3, -63, and -71.7 dB
  for 4, 2.5, and 1 cm aperture, respectively.
- FOV changes over the tested range produce less than about 0.2 dB in the
  illustrated condition.
- Larger pole spacing worsens the weakest part of the periodic coverage.
- Lateral shift has small effect on average in the tested symmetric highway
  geometry.
- The approximate BER with at least five quadrature terms closely tracks
  Monte Carlo simulation.
- For BER \(10^{-3}\), the paper reports required “transmit SNR” values around
  159, 141.9, and 135 dB for 1, 2.5, and 4 cm apertures. The apparently huge
  values follow the paper's transmit-SNR definition before propagation loss;
  they must not be mistaken for receiver SNR.

### Limitations

- I2V streetlights, not V2V headlights/taillights.
- Static ideal highway geometry; no interacting vehicle mobility.
- No vehicles modeled as optical blockers.
- Clear weather only in the principal fit.
- One luminaire family and bounded fitted geometry.
- PHY/channel and BER study, not a network/link-control evaluation.

### Coding relevance

**Our inference:** W18 supplies a useful periodic infrastructure-link model and
shows that aperture and pole spacing deserve explicit sensitivity analysis.
It should not be transplanted as the V2V channel. It can serve as an optional
I2V extension or as evidence that infrastructure geometry produces
predictable, nonstationary coverage.

## W19 — A Proposal for VLC-Assisting IEEE 802.11p Communication for Vehicular Environment Using a Prediction-Based Handover

### Citation and access

- M. Y. Abualhoul, M. Al-Bado, O. Shagdar, and F. Nashashibi,
  “A Proposal for VLC-Assisting IEEE802.11p Communication for Vehicular
  Environment Using a Prediction-based Handover,” in *2018 21st International
  Conference on Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITSC)*, Maui, 2018.
- HAL record: <https://inria.hal.science/hal-01888576>
- Local full text: `research/literature/pdfs/W19.pdf`
- Access depth: full author manuscript.

### Architecture

The system targets vehicle platoons:

- IEEE 802.11p continues broadcasting ordinary vehicular messages;
- directional VLC is preferred for intra-platoon communication;
- RF unicast is activated on demand before a predicted VLC interruption;
- RF and VLC overlap for a dwell/redundancy period to avoid a service gap and
  rapid chatter.

The approach is decentralized and heuristic, not reinforcement learning.

### Interruption detection

The paper uses several checks:

- packet-sequence gap, with a reported threshold \(\Delta N \ge 4\);
- received-length mismatch;
- relative vehicle orientation/geometry; and
- immediate fallback for obstacle/packet-fault conditions.

The stated packet interval is at most 33 ms. Three missed packets imply about
99 ms, selected relative to a 100 ms safety-message period/requirement.

### Prediction rule

Using positions and headings, the controller computes front/rear relative
angles. It defines a critical optical angle \(\theta_c=15^\circ\) and a
speed-dependent predictive threshold

\[
\theta_p
 = \theta_c\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\frac{v^2}{v_{\max}^2}\right).
\]

When the maximum relevant orientation angle exceeds \(\theta_p\), the RF link
is activated in anticipation of the VLC path leaving the FOV. The threshold
shrinks as speed rises, creating earlier redundancy.

This is “prediction” in the sense of a deterministic speed/geometry heuristic,
not a learned forecast with uncertainty.

### Chatter protection

The system waits about 500 ms of stable conditions before turning off the RF
backup. This hysteresis/dwell time is a major part of the behavior and cost; it
should not be omitted when reproducing the algorithm.

### Simulation

- ns-3 WAVE/IEEE 802.11p plus a modified point-to-point VLC module;
- SUMO 0.26 mobility;
- road length 10 km;
- four bidirectional lanes;
- 600 vehicles;
- 22 platoons of four vehicles;
- nominal intra-platoon gap 8 m (one table/description also creates an
  ambiguous 24 m quantity that should be checked carefully when reproducing);
- background speeds sampled around 10-20 m/s;
- platoon speed around 12 m/s;
- 100 ms message generation;
- RF coverage around 500 m.

### Results

- Hybrid/prediction-based operation improves delivery by roughly 10-25%
  depending on the dense scenario; the body highlights gains up to about 20%.
- The forced-turn experiments report 100% interruption prediction under the
  tested settings.
- At speeds above about 15 m/s, the speed-dependent rule creates around
  300 ms of redundancy in an illustrated comparison.

These results demonstrate the heuristic in the authors' generated events;
“100% prediction” is not an out-of-distribution safety guarantee.

### Limitations

The authors themselves identify needed extensions for channel busy ratio,
MAC/PHY behavior, reflection, optical interference, ambient light, and
weather. Additional limitations:

- high-accuracy position/heading is assumed;
- deterministic turn geometry;
- no uncertainty calibration;
- IEEE 802.11p rather than NR sidelink;
- no formal cost/reliability constraint;
- no learning or long-horizon control.

### Coding relevance

W19 is a strong **rule-based hybrid baseline**. A faithful implementation
should include packet-loss detection, relative-angle prediction, speed-aware
early activation, and the 500 ms release dwell. It is also direct precedent
for duplication as a transient action rather than only a permanent mode.

## W20 — INVISIBLE 2.0: An Enhanced Interference-Based Handover Technique for Visible Light Communications in Vehicular Networks

### Citation and access

- M. Mayahi, V. Loscrí, and A. M. Vegni, “INVISIBLE 2.0: An Enhanced
  Interference-Based Handover Technique for Visible Light Communications in
  Vehicular Networks,” *Computer Networks*, vol. 242, art. 110280, 2024.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1016/j.comnet.2024.110280>
- HAL manuscript: <https://hal.science/hal-04476693>
- Local full text: `research/literature/pdfs/W20.pdf`
- Access depth: full accepted manuscript.

### Architecture

INVISIBLE 2.0 coordinates VLC with LTE C-V2X:

- VLC is the preferred local link where quality permits;
- **horizontal handover** moves between visible-light access points;
- **vertical handover** falls back to LTE;
- the decision explicitly examines whether poor optical performance is caused
  by one dominant optical interferer.

It considers both V2V and V2I cases in urban and highway settings.

### Decision logic

The controller is triggered by a reliability/error condition. For an optical
candidate, it computes:

- candidate SNR;
- total interference-to-noise ratio (INR);
- interference-impact ratio
  \[
  \mathrm{IIR}
    = \frac{\max_j P_{\mathrm{interferer},j}}
            {\sum_j P_{\mathrm{interferer},j}};
  \]
- a dominance threshold \(\beta\).

If interference is significant (the text uses total INR above 10), one
dominant interferer exists (\(\mathrm{IIR}\ge\beta\)), and an alternate VLC
candidate meets its SNR requirement, horizontal handover is used. Otherwise,
LTE is selected.

### Reliability-threshold ambiguity

The prose states a maximum error probability of \(10^{-3}\) and a target PRR
of at least 95%. However, its algorithm/table language also describes a PER
threshold as “95%” and uses inconsistent inequality wording in places.

This is a genuine notation/unit-direction ambiguity in the paper. A
reimplementation must define:

- whether the trigger variable is PER, success probability, or PRR;
- whether crossing occurs above or below the threshold; and
- separate values for a \(10^{-3}\) PHY error target and a 95% packet-delivery
  target.

Those quantities are not interchangeable.

### Energy model

The paper decomposes transmit/receive/sense/idle consumption and reports
energy efficiency in bits/J. Component tables include RF DSP, analog,
DAC/ADC, and optical LED/TIA-related terms. This makes it useful as a source
for **relative accounting structure**.

The authors caution that absolute power values require measurement. The model
should therefore be parameterized rather than copied as universal hardware
truth.

### Simulation

- ns-3 VLC extensions integrated with ms-van3t LTE;
- ETSI-style CAM generation based on movement, speed, heading, or a one-second
  maximum interval;
- urban Manhattan and highway scenarios;
- urban speeds about 45-72 km/h;
- highway speeds about 90-105 km/h;
- optical pole spacing about 23 m urban and 25 m highway;
- OOK;
- traffic density about 10-110 vehicles/km;
- LTE modeled with WINNER+ B1/Release-14-era settings.

### Results

Results are largely read from figures:

- the hybrid mechanism maintains at least roughly 95% PRR in the tested
  cases;
- LTE V2I is the most robust V2I reference;
- in highway V2I, VLC is unusable under the paper's settings and the hybrid
  method effectively remains on LTE;
- hybrid V2V delay improves by about an order of magnitude relative to
  C-V2X in favorable VLC conditions;
- urban V2I latency improves when VLC can be exploited, while highway V2I
  mirrors LTE;
- energy-efficiency trends vary by sender/receiver and density; the hybrid
  method usually improves transmitter efficiency except where it remains on
  LTE.

The result prose contains at least one internally awkward/contradictory
“best/lowest” description, so exact plotted values should be digitized from
the final paper before using them as regression-test targets.

### Limitations

- Deterministic heuristic, not RL/CMDP.
- LTE Release 14 rather than NR sidelink.
- Simplified Lambertian LOS optical channel.
- No explicit vehicle blockage, weather, coding, or learned forecast.
- No explicit action-switching cost in the optimization sense.
- Some threshold notation and result prose are ambiguous.

### Coding relevance

W20 is the closest **non-learning hybrid vehicular handover benchmark** in this
batch. Reproduce the decision tree and energy accounting, but normalize its
threshold semantics first. Its highway-V2I result is an important falsifier:
a good hybrid controller must be willing to stay on RF when the optical
opportunity is structurally absent.

## W21 — Distinguishing Micromobility and Blockage in 6G Sub-THz Systems: A Machine Learning Approach

### Citation and access

- H. A. Jeba, A. Gaydamaka, and D. Moltchanov, “Distinguishing Micromobility
  and Blockage in 6G Sub-THz Systems: A Machine Learning Approach,”
  *IEEE Open Journal of the Communications Society*, vol. 6,
  pp. 7810-7822, 2025.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/OJCOMS.2025.3599666>
- Local full text: `research/literature/pdfs/W21.pdf`
- Access depth: full CC-BY paper.

### Problem

The same received-power outage can require different actions:

- human/body blockage: search/switch base station or use multi-connectivity;
- device micromobility/misalignment: track or realign the beam.

Using the wrong action prolongs outage or wastes network resources. The paper
therefore classifies the **cause** of a high-frequency outage rather than only
predicting that received power is low.

### Measurements

#### Blockage

- 156 GHz measurement link;
- approximately 7.5 m by 2.4 m by 3 m hall;
- blocker walking around 3.5 km/h;
- Tx-Rx separation 3, 5, and 7 m;
- multiple blocker positions;
- terminal heights 1.35 and 1.65 m;
- 30 repetitions per configuration;
- transmit power 90 mW;
- 25 dB horn antennas with about 10° half-power beamwidth;
- 50 microsecond sampling.

#### Micromobility

- application motion classes: racing, phone use, VR, and video;
- separate 44 mW link at 4 m;
- yaw/pitch motion through goniometers, maximum about 80°/s;
- 30 traces;
- VR/racing treated as higher-mobility patterns and phone/video as lower.

### Joint-signal construction

The experiment did not measure blockage and micromobility jointly with an
electronically steerable setup. Instead, the paper:

- measures them separately;
- fits/constructs micromobility traces;
- generates blockage arrivals using a stochastic
  geometry/arrival model;
- superimposes measured/fitted blockage slopes and variability; and
- models regular or on-demand beam tracking.

The resulting “joint impairment” dataset is therefore synthetic composition
of measured components, not a joint field measurement.

### Detection concept

The paper observes pre-blockage received-power oscillation:

- oscillation followed by outage implies blockage and a BS-switch action;
- outage without the precursor is classified as micromobility and invokes
  beam tracking.

The ML models are decision tree, random forest, XGBoost, autoencoder, and a
Conv1D/LSTM architecture.

### Dataset and model details

- 480 samples;
- each raw sample has about 1,500 received-signal-power features;
- binary labels distinguish the precursor/non-blockage condition used by the
  detection pipeline;
- random forest: 100 trees;
- decision tree depth: 3;
- XGBoost: 100 estimators;
- autoencoder latent dimension: 32, trained for 100 epochs with Adam at 0.001;
- LSTM: 64 units and dropout 0.4.

### Results

- Random forest reaches about 95% classification accuracy in the composed
  joint-impairment scenario.
- XGBoost is around 90%; the Conv1D/LSTM is around 80% in the reported
  comparison.
- Random forest has high blockage recall and relatively few false negatives,
  the costly error in which blockage is treated as micromobility.
- Training is reported as roughly 10-20 s on an Apple M2-class CPU; this is
  offline training time, not control-loop latency.

### Limitations and validity risks

- Joint impairment is synthetically composed.
- Blockage and micromobility use different measurement setups/powers.
- Small sample count with 1,500-dimensional raw inputs.
- The paper does not clearly document a robust grouped train/test split or
  cross-validation protocol; repeated/traces from the same configuration
  could create leakage. This is a serious generalization caveat.
- No multi-blocker, external interference, rich reflection, nonlinear human
  motion, or end-to-end power/resource controller.
- Sub-THz user-to-BS, not vehicular VLC.
- Classification accuracy is not the final reliability of the handover
  policy.

### Coding relevance

**Our inference:** W21 motivates a cause-aware belief or forecast head:
blockage, misalignment, and interference should not be collapsed into one
scalar SNR. But classifier outputs must remain probabilistic/calibrated, and
splits must be grouped by route/seed/scenario to prevent leakage. Do not insert
ground-truth outage causes into an RL observation unless a real estimator
could observe them.

## W22 — Deep-Reinforcement-Learning-Based Mode Selection and Resource Allocation for Cellular V2X Communications

### Citation and access

- X. Zhang, M. Peng, S. Yan, and Y. Sun,
  “Deep-Reinforcement-Learning-Based Mode Selection and Resource Allocation
  for Cellular V2X Communications,” *IEEE Internet of Things Journal*,
  vol. 7, no. 7, pp. 6380-6391, 2020.
- DOI: <https://doi.org/10.1109/JIOT.2019.2962715>
- Primary preprint: <https://arxiv.org/abs/2002.05485>
- Local full text: `research/literature/pdfs/W22.pdf`
- Access depth: full paper, including equations, algorithms, parameter tables,
  and result figures.

### System

One base station sits at a road intersection. Users are divided into:

- \(M\) I-VUEs that send high-throughput uplink traffic to the BS; and
- \(K\) transmitter-receiver V2V pairs that carry safety messages.

There are \(F\) resource blocks. Each I-VUE occupies one uplink RB; a V2V pair
can reuse an occupied RB or use a currently unused one.

Each V2V pair selects:

1. direct V2V mode; or
2. indirect V2I mode, uploading to the BS and then using a downlink.

In the paper's abstraction, the V2I-mode rate is approximately one half of the
uplink capacity, representing the two-hop path.

### Optimization

The global objective maximizes sum I-VUE capacity subject to:

- minimum I-VUE capacity;
- V2V transmission-latency requirement;
- V2V outage/reliability requirement;
- binary direct/indirect mode;
- one RB per V2V pair;
- at most one indirect V2I-mode V2V pair on an unused RB; and
- maximum transmit power.

Only large-scale channel gain is assumed known for control, while fast fading
is handled statistically.

### Important equation error

The paper defines the outage condition

\[
\Pr\{\gamma_k^v\le\gamma_o\}\le p_o.
\]

Under its Rayleigh-fading argument, the required mean/large-scale SINR should
satisfy

\[
\gamma_k^v \ge
\frac{\gamma_o}{\ln(1/(1-p_o))}.
\]

Equation (10) in the published/preprint text prints the relation with
\(\le\), which is physically and algebraically reversed. The reward later uses
\(G(\gamma_k^v-\gamma_{\mathrm{eff}})\), i.e. it rewards SINR **above** the
effective threshold, confirming the intended direction.

This must be corrected in any implementation.

### Decentralized DQN formulation

Each V2V pair is an agent.

#### State

At subframe \(t\), the local state contains:

- previous interference at its V2V receiver on every RB;
- previous interference at the BS on every RB;
- previous number of neighboring V2V selections on every RB;
- current large-scale gain of its direct V2V link;
- current large-scale gain from its transmitter to the BS;
- remaining message load; and
- remaining time before the latency deadline.

#### Action

\[
a_t = \{\text{RB},\ \text{mode},\ \text{power}\}.
\]

Mode is binary, the RB is one of \(F\), and power is one of \(N_p\) discrete
levels from zero through \(P_{\max}\), giving \(2FN_p\) actions.

#### Reward

The shared reward adds:

1. I-VUE sum-capacity revenue;
2. a term rewarding/penalizing satisfaction of minimum I-VUE capacity;
3. a term rewarding/penalizing V2V reliability-threshold satisfaction; and
4. a term rewarding/penalizing whether achievable V2V rate can clear the
   remaining load in the remaining time.

The piecewise function \(G(x)\) returns a fixed positive constant when a
constraint margin is nonnegative and returns the negative margin otherwise.
Reward weights are \(0.1, 0.9, 1, 1\) in the reported simulation.

Although each action is local, computing the shared reward requires
communication: V2V pairs broadcast remaining load and experienced SINR, and
I-VUEs broadcast experienced rate. The controller is therefore not
communication-free or fully independent.

### Learning

- ordinary DQN, experience replay, fixed target network, epsilon-greedy;
- one fully connected hidden layer with 256 ReLU units;
- Adam optimizer;
- learning rate 0.001;
- discount 0.70;
- epsilon 1 to 0.01 over 1,000 exploration steps;
- replay memory 3,000;
- minibatch 8;
- online-network update every 2 steps;
- target update every 30 steps;
- 10 steps per epoch.

### Two-timescale federated DRL

#### Large timescale

The BS builds a weighted graph from large-scale inter-vehicle channel gains
and uses spectral clustering. Candidate RB groups exclude resources allocated
to interfering I-VUEs in the same cluster.

#### Small timescale

- V2V agents in a cluster train local DQNs;
- models are averaged at the BS every 100 steps;
- the averaged cluster model is redistributed;
- action updates are made asynchronously within a cluster to reduce
  simultaneous collisions;
- a newly activated V2V pair downloads its cluster's global model rather than
  training from scratch.

This is “semi-decentralized”: action selection is local, but the BS performs
clustering and federated aggregation.

### Simulation

- crossroad: 1 km by 1 km, two lanes per direction;
- Poisson vehicle placement;
- one center BS;
- 5 I-VUEs;
- 5, 10, 15, or 20 V2V pairs;
- V2V receiver chosen as the farthest vehicle within broadcast range;
- 3GPP TR 37.885-style urban LOS/NLOS, path loss, shadowing, and fading;
- 2 GHz carrier;
- 10 RBs, 180 kHz each;
- vehicle speed 36 km/h;
- V2V separation set/reported as 150 m;
- maximum vehicle transmit power 23 dBm;
- noise power -114 dBm;
- 5 clusters.

For the primary evaluation, an 800-byte message must be delivered in 10 ms at
99% reliability with a 3 dB outage threshold; I-VUE capacity requirement is
3 bit/s/Hz.

The introduction also cites a different external example (1,200 bytes, 5 ms,
99.999%). It is not the simulated requirement and should not be reported as
such.

### Baselines and results

Baselines:

- centralized mode/resource optimization using a greedy mode step plus
  Hungarian/closed-form allocation and assumed global CSI;
- decentralized DQN with direct V2V mode only;
- random RB choice from a five-RB lower-interference pool at maximum power.

Reported findings:

- joint mode selection improves I-VUE sum capacity and V2V QoS-satisfaction
  rate over the direct-only and random decentralized methods;
- the advantage grows with V2V-pair count because the agent can move NLOS/
  unstable pairs to the indirect mode;
- the decentralized DQN approaches the centralized benchmark in the tested
  range;
- increasing the outage threshold decreases both I-VUE capacity and
  V2V-satisfaction rate;
- the federated model converges near the centralized reward;
- for a newly activated pair, downloading the cluster model gives immediate,
  stable performance, whereas transfer-from-nearest and scratch models need
  exploration/training; their eventual performance is similar.

The paper's plots support these relative conclusions. They do not report
confidence intervals, multiple independent seed distributions, or rare-event
upper bounds.

### Limitations

- RF cellular V2V/V2I mode selection, not RF/VLC.
- Stylized crossroad and fixed-speed Poisson placement, not a coupled
  microscopic traffic simulation.
- Reliability modeled through a fading/outage threshold, not empirical
  deadline-miss tail probability.
- Printed constraint-sign error in Eq. (10).
- Shared global reward requires broadcasts and ignores their overhead/errors.
- Nonstationarity remains despite asynchronous updates.
- Small replay/batch settings and no seed-level uncertainty reporting.
- 99% simulated reliability is far from demonstrating \(10^{-5}\)-level
  violation probability.
- Federated averaging assumes parameter-compatible agents and sufficiently
  similar cluster tasks; adversarial, non-IID, stale, or lossy updates are not
  evaluated.

### Coding relevance

W22 is a strong structural precursor for:

- mode + resource + power action factorization;
- remaining-load and remaining-time observations;
- direct versus infrastructure-assisted communication;
- local execution with a coordination layer; and
- new-agent model transfer.

For the planned hybrid RF/VLC problem, its soft shared reward should be
replaced or augmented by explicit cost signals/CMDP accounting, and its
reliability equation must be corrected. The communication required to build
the state/reward must be included in the observability and overhead model.

## Cross-entry synthesis from W17-W22

### A useful layered interpretation

```text
W17/W18: optical physics and validity domain
W19/W20: deterministic hybrid switching and duplication baselines
W21:     latent outage-cause estimation
W22:     learned mode/resource/power control with deadline context
```

No one paper supplies all four layers.

### Most defensible implementation transfer

- Build optical opportunity from geometry, orientation, FOV, blocker, ambient,
  and weather variables (W17).
- Keep infrastructure periodicity separate from direct V2V optics (W18).
- Implement predictive angle/hysteresis and interference-based horizontal/
  vertical decision rules as baselines (W19/W20).
- Estimate an outage-cause probability rather than exposing simulator truth
  (W21).
- Include remaining bytes/time and factorized mode/resource/power actions
  (W22).

### Validation rules implied by this batch

- Report both communication performance and switching/duplication cost.
- Separate mean delay from deadline-miss probability.
- Use route/seed/scenario-grouped train/test splits.
- Evaluate no-VLC-opportunity regimes, because an optimal hybrid policy may
  legitimately choose RF continuously.
- Correct and unit-test analytical constraint transformations before using
  them as reward terms.
- Treat an optical model as a family of assumptions; report sensitivity to
  radiation pattern, aperture, FOV, blockers, and weather.
